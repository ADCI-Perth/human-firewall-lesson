## People are part of the defence

In [The insider threat](04-insider-threat.md) you met Colwill (2009), who writes about the insider threat. His abstract says that technical controls must not be used in isolation, and that questions of loyalty arise when an organisation is going through change. This episode takes the same paper's concern, the people side of security, into everyday workplace questions.

### Social media at work

Social media at work raises three people questions.

- What may staff say about the organisation?
- What must they not share?
- What happens when someone is unhappy?

A rule that says only "never post about work" is hard to follow and easy to ignore. Better guidance is specific. Do not share customer details. Do not share anything confidential. And here is who to ask if you are not sure. Explain the reasons, train people, and make it safe to say, "I made a mistake." The NCSC has a page for organisations on protecting what they publish, which is listed under Go deeper below.

### Unhappy staff and change

When someone is unhappy, or the organisation is changing, the risk goes up. A staff member who feels ignored may post a complaint online, or worse, take information with them. Managers who notice, listen and treat people fairly lower that risk far more than any filter can. A filter does not deal with why the person is upset.

### What an employer may reasonably do

Employers have a legitimate interest. They own the work systems, and they are responsible for the information on them. So it is reasonable for an employer to set rules for work computers, and to log activity on them. The test has three parts.

1. **Proportionate.** Is the control in proportion to the risk?
2. **Open.** Are staff told what is watched, and why?
3. **About work.** Does it stay focused on work?

An employer who secretly reads private messages, or demands the passwords to your personal accounts, has gone past that test. Staff have duties too: follow the rules, report problems early, and think before you post.

## What tighter security costs

Every control costs someone something. Passwords that expire weekly, approval for every app, a ban on personal phones. Each can make the organisation safer, and each can make work slower. When the cost is too high, people find workarounds. They email files to themselves, use free apps nobody approved, or leave sessions open. A control that people go around can be worse than none, because the organisation believes it is protected.

So ask two questions of any rule.

- **How much security does it add?**
- **How much burden does it put on staff?**

Good policy keeps the first high and the second low. In the first challenge you will score rules on both.

### BYOD: bring your own device

**BYOD** stands for bring your own device. Staff use their own phones or laptops for work. The benefits are lower cost and familiar tools. The risks are these.

- Personal and work data mix.
- The device can be lost.
- It may be too old to receive security updates.
- Work files may copy into a personal cloud account.
- The organisation does not own the device, so it cannot simply inspect or wipe it.

Sensible controls include a screen lock and encryption, an approved list of devices that still receive updates, and keeping work data in a separate work app or profile. Then a lost phone can have the work data removed without touching the owner's personal photos. There should also be a clear rule for when someone leaves: the work accounts and data are removed from the phone, and the owner's personal data is left alone.

Volunteers are a special case. They are not employees, and an organisation has less claim on their phones. The same logic applies, only more strongly: ask for as little as the risk needs.

### Working from home or off-site

The office has managed networks, locked doors and colleagues nearby. At home, the network is yours: perhaps a router still using its default password, and updates that keep getting postponed. In a café, there is public Wi-Fi, and people who can see your screen or hear your calls.

Useful controls are the organisation's secure remote access, a private place to work, devices kept up to date, and screens locked when you step away. Your responsibilities are to follow the rules, keep work separate from home life, and report a lost device or an odd message quickly, without fear of blame.

## Challenge: Score Riverbend's draft rules (15 minutes)

**The situation.** Riverbend's volunteers use their own phones, and the roster is organised through a group chat. This episode adds two details for the exercise. Some paid staff would like to prepare the monthly newsletter at home, using the free online tool that holds the mailing list. The library manager has written two short draft policies, one for each situation. They are drafts only. Nobody has agreed to them.

**Draft 1: Volunteers' phones and the roster**

| # | Rule |
|---|---|
| 1.1 | Volunteers may keep using their own phones for the roster. The group chat is for shifts and changes only. |
| 1.2 | Do not put children's names, allergies or members' details in the group chat, or in any message or photo on your phone. If you need to pass one on, speak to the library manager or a senior staff member. |
| 1.3 | Every volunteer must install an app chosen by the library. It lets the library see the phone's apps, messages and location, so the library can check the roster. |
| 1.4 | When a volunteer stops volunteering, the library removes them from the group chat on their last day. |

**Draft 2: Working from home**

| # | Rule |
|---|---|
| 2.1 | Paid staff may prepare the newsletter at home on their own computer, and are expected to install updates promptly. |
| 2.2 | The mailing list may be opened only on a library laptop. Staff collect it at the start of each working day and return it to the library at the end. |
| 2.3 | To sign in to the mailing-list tool, staff enter a code from a phone app as well as their password. |
| 2.4 | If you lose a phone or computer used for library work, tell the library manager as soon as you notice. |

Use the [Riverbend page](../learners/riverbend.md) for facts about the library and the drafts above for what each rule says. If you need a detail that neither gives, say what you are assuming.

1. **Read the page and the drafts** (3 minutes).
2. **Score every rule** (12 minutes). Copy the table below, with 8 rows, one for each rule. Give each rule two scores from **1 to 5**:
   - **Security gained:** 1 = adds very little protection, 5 = adds a lot.
   - **Burden on staff or volunteers:** 1 = very light, 5 = very heavy.

   Add **one short reason** for each pair of scores. Only use what the rule says. If it does not say something, do not assume it.

   | Rule | Security gained (1–5) | Burden (1–5) | Reason |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | 1.1 | | | |

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

One worked answer. A score one point higher or lower is sound if your reason fits the rule's words.

| Rule | Security gained | Burden | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | 2 | 1 | Volunteers carry on as they do now, so the burden is almost nil. Limiting the chat to shifts and changes helps a little, but the rule does not say how this is kept to. |
| 1.2 | 4 | 2 | It keeps children's details and members' details out of a chat on personal phones. The Riverbend page says the children's program records allergies and photo consent, so these are details worth keeping out. The cost is a conversation with the manager or a senior staff member when a detail must be passed on. |
| 1.3 | 3 | 5 | The library could see everything on a volunteer's phone, including personal messages and location, which goes far beyond the roster. It is hard to see how this is in proportion to the risk, and it is not just about library work. Volunteers give their time freely, so many might refuse. The rule does not say what the library would do with what it sees. |
| 1.4 | 4 | 1 | Removing a person from the chat on their last day cuts off their access to later shifts and changes. The cost is small. The rule does not say what happens to anything already sent to that person's phone. |
| 2.1 | 2 | 1 | Asking staff to install updates is a good habit but checks nothing. There is nothing about a screen lock or about where the mailing list may be kept. |
| 2.2 | 4 | 4 | Only library-owned laptops open the list, so a lot less could leak from home computers. But staff must collect and return the laptop every working day, which defeats much of the point of working from home. The rule does not say what staff do if they cannot get to the library. |
| 2.3 | 5 | 2 | A second check at each sign-in means a stolen password alone is not enough, for a few seconds at each login. The rule assumes staff have a phone that can run the app. |
| 2.4 | 3 | 1 | Quick reporting limits the damage of a loss and costs almost nothing. But the rule only says to tell the manager. It does not say what the library would then do. |

## Challenge: Weigh the trade and improve the rules (10 minutes)

Use your scores from the previous challenge. Write 2 to 4 sentences for each of these.

1. **Worst and best trade** (4 minutes). Which rule is the worst trade (a heavy burden for little security gained)? Which is the best trade?
2. **Workarounds** (3 minutes). What might staff or volunteers do if a rule's burden is very high? What would that do to the security gained?
3. **Rewrite** (3 minutes). Choose **one rule from Draft 1** and **one rule from Draft 2**. Rewrite each so that the balance improves. Say what you changed and why.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

One worked answer.

1. **Worst and best trade.** The worst trade is rule 1.3. It asks volunteers to give the library a view of their phones, and it gains less than it costs. Rule 2.2 is close, because it gains security but takes away most of the benefit of working from home. The best trade is rule 2.3: a code from a phone app costs a few seconds and protects the mailing list even if a password is stolen. Rule 1.2 is also a good trade. Either 2.3 or 1.2 is a sound choice if you say why.
2. **Workarounds.** Volunteers might refuse the app, or leave the group chat for another way to swap shifts. Staff might email the mailing list to themselves so they do not have to carry the laptop. Each workaround breaks the protection the rule was meant to give. The library would believe it was protected, and it would not be.
3. **Rewrites (examples).**
   - *Rule 1.3:* "The library sets up a roster app or chat that it manages. Volunteers use that for shifts, and the library can remove a volunteer's access to it. The library cannot see anything else on a volunteer's phone." This keeps the part the library needs, control over its own roster, and removes the part that reaches into personal phones.
   - *Rule 2.2:* "Staff may open the mailing list at home on a computer that is kept up to date and has a screen lock. They sign in with a password and a code, and do not save the list to the computer." This asks for a small habit in place of carrying a laptop, and it still protects the list.
   - *Rule 2.1:* "Staff who work at home keep their computer up to date and use a screen lock." This is specific, so it is easier to follow and to check.
   - *Rule 1.4:* "On a volunteer's last day, the library removes them from the group chat and asks them to delete any library details from their phone." This closes the gap the rule left open.
   - Any rewrite is sound if it improves the balance and you say why.

**An overall point.** Neither draft gets the balance right everywhere. Draft 1 asks little of volunteers in some rules, then asks far too much in one. Draft 2 has a strong, light rule in 2.3, but its strictest rule makes working from home hard. Better policies keep the burden low and the protection high, for example with a separate work app, a small sign-in code and specific, explained rules.

### Common mistakes

- **Scoring only the security side.** Both scores matter. A strict rule can be a poor trade if the burden is heavy.
- **Thinking strict means safe.** A rule that people work around can protect less than a lighter rule that people follow.
- **Thinking light means friendly.** A rule that asks almost nothing can still cost staff, members and children a great deal if something goes wrong.
- **Assuming facts that are not in the rule.** The drafts do not say how the app would be used, what the library would do after a loss, or how anyone would check the rules. Score what is written, and say "the rule does not say".
- **Giving a score with no reason.** One short reason that points to the rule's words is enough.
- **Rewriting a rule by deleting it.** A better rule keeps the useful part and lowers the burden.
- **Adding facts about Riverbend.** Use the Riverbend page and the drafts above. If you need a detail, say it is an assumption.

## Quick check (5 minutes)

1. Odile resigns from her job. She has used her own phone for work email and files. What should the organisation's policy say happens on her last day?

   - A. Nothing, because she is a trusted former colleague.
   - B. The whole phone is wiped, including her personal photos and messages.
   - C. The work accounts and data are removed from the phone, and her personal data is left alone.
   - D. She is asked to delete the work email app herself within a month.

2. Emeka's own tablet is nine years old and no longer receives security updates from its maker. He asks to use it for work email under the company's BYOD policy. What is the best response?

   - A. Allow it, because it works well and has a screen lock set up.
   - B. Decline it for work and explain why, then offer a supported or loan device instead.
   - C. Allow it for a one-month trial, then review whether it is still safe to use.
   - D. Allow it, as long as he promises not to open any attachments in his work email.

3. A company asks staff who use their own phones for work to install software that can read all their personal messages and track their location around the clock. What is the best way to handle this?

   - A. Install it on every phone, because the company owns all the work data.
   - B. Install it, and promise to read messages only if someone complains about that particular staff member.
   - C. Allow personal phones only for staff who agree to be tracked by the company during all of their working hours.
   - D. Limit control to a separate work app, tell staff what can be seen, and leave personal data alone.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

1. **C.** Work data left on a former employee's device is a risk, and wiping her personal photos would be unfair. Trusting her does not remove the data, and asking her to delete the app within a month leaves the data on the phone for weeks.
2. **B.** An approved list should include only devices that still receive security updates, and offering an alternative keeps the burden on Emeka low. A screen lock does not fix known weaknesses that will never be patched. A trial month still leaves work data on a device that cannot be kept up to date. A promise not to open attachments is not a control, because many weaknesses can be reached without opening one.
3. **D.** This passes the three tests: it is proportionate, staff are told what is watched and why, and it stays focused on work. Reading all personal messages and tracking location around the clock goes far beyond what protecting work data needs. A promise to read only after a complaint still collects far more than the risk needs.

## Reflect (5 minutes)

Write 3 to 6 sentences. Do not name other people or share private details such as passwords, card numbers or account details.

- **Work, devices and control.** How much control should an employer have over what you do on a work computer or network? Where would you draw the line, using the three tests (proportionate, open and about work)? Should you have to use your own phone for work? What would make you comfortable, or not, about doing it? Why is working from home, or from somewhere else, harder to secure than working in an office, and what do you think your own responsibilities would be to your employer?

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: instructor

**Timing (55 minutes).** Teaching text 20 minutes. Exercises 35 minutes: score Riverbend's draft rules 15, weigh the trade and improve the rules 10, quick check 5, discussion 5. Ask learners to read the [Riverbend page](../learners/riverbend.md) before the session, or give 3 minutes at the start of the first challenge.

**Likely misconceptions.**

- Learners think strict means safe. Ask what a volunteer or staff member would do if the rule made the job much harder.
- Learners think light means friendly. Ask who pays if a lost phone holds children's allergy details.
- Learners score only one column. Hold them to both scores and a reason.
- Learners add facts to Riverbend or to the drafts, such as how the library would use the app. Ask them to say "the rule does not say" or "I am assuming".
- Learners rewrite a rule by deleting it. Ask what the library would lose.
- Learners treat volunteers like employees. Ask what claim an organisation has on a volunteer's own phone.

**Running the discussion.** Use think-pair-share. The question about using your own phone for work is personal, so ask pairs to share the *condition* that would make them comfortable and not their own phone settings. Ask pairs who disagree about where to draw the line to apply the three tests to the same example.

**Tip.** Put the eight rules on a board and ask each pair to place a sticky note for each rule on a grid with security gained across and burden up. The rules in the bottom-right corner (high security, low burden) are the good trades. Discuss the ones in the top-left.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: keypoints

- People are part of the defence. Technical controls on their own are not enough, and managers who notice, listen and treat people fairly lower the risk more than any filter.
- Specific, explained social media guidance works better than "never post about work". Say what not to share and who to ask, and make it safe to admit a mistake.
- An employer's control passes the test if it is proportionate to the risk, open to the staff it affects, and about work.
- Ask two questions of every rule: how much security does it add, and how much burden does it put on people? Rules that are too heavy get worked around.
- BYOD and working from home need clear rules: updated devices, a separate work app or profile, secure remote access, quick reporting, and removing work data when someone leaves.
