## The Privacy Act and the APPs

The main privacy law is the *Privacy Act 1988*. It is a Commonwealth law. Its purpose is to protect the privacy of individuals and to set rules for how personal information is handled.

The heart of the Act is 13 **Australian Privacy Principles**, or APPs. The OAIC (n.d.a) says they apply to some private sector organisations and to most Australian Government agencies. Together these are called **APP entities**.

Which private organisations are covered?

- Generally, those with an annual turnover of more than three million dollars.
- Many small businesses are outside the Act.
- One important exception is this. A private **health service provider** that holds health information is covered even if it is small (OAIC n.d.e). That includes GPs, dentists, pharmacists and allied health professionals. A small clinic cannot say it is too small to matter.

Health information is a kind of **sensitive information**. The law asks for extra care with sensitive information, because the harm from its exposure can be much greater.

### Which law covers a council-run library?

Riverbend Community Library is run by a local council. A local council is not a Commonwealth agency, and it is generally covered by the privacy law of its state or territory rather than by the Privacy Act. Those state and territory laws have their own principles. They ask many of the same questions as the APPs, but the wording, the coverage and the rules about reporting breaches differ from place to place. The [Riverbend page](../learners/riverbend.md) does not say which state the library is in.

So this episode does **not** claim that the APPs legally bind Riverbend. In the challenges you will **treat Riverbend as if it were covered by the APPs**. That is a teaching device. The APPs are the national set of principles, and the questions they ask (what do you collect, what do you tell people, how do you use it, how do you keep it safe, what do people get back) are questions any privacy law asks. In real life, the first step is to find out which law applies to the organisation in front of you.

### Sorting the APPs by stage

You do not need to memorise thirteen numbers. Ask instead: at what stage of the information's life did the problem happen?

- **Collecting:** what did the organisation ask for, and did it explain why?
- **Using and sharing:** did it use or pass on information for a purpose it should not have?
- **Keeping safe:** did it protect the information, and delete it when it was no longer needed?
- **Open and fair:** can people find out how the organisation handles information, see what it holds and ask for corrections?

The nine APPs used in the challenges are below. These are plain-language summaries, based on the OAIC's quick reference (OAIC n.d.a). The OAIC page lists all 13. The ones not shown are APP 2 (anonymity and pseudonymity), APP 4 (unsolicited information), APP 9 (government related identifiers) and APP 10 (quality of information).

| APP | Name | What it asks, in plain words |
|---|---|---|
| APP 1 | Open and transparent management of personal information | Have a clear, up-to-date privacy policy, make it available, and have practices and systems to follow the APPs and handle privacy questions and complaints |
| APP 3 | Collection of solicited personal information | Collect only what is reasonably necessary for what the organisation does, with higher standards for sensitive information |
| APP 5 | Notification of the collection of personal information | When you collect information, take reasonable steps to tell people who you are, why you are collecting it and who it usually goes to |
| APP 6 | Use or disclosure of personal information | Use or share information only for the purpose it was collected, unless an exception applies (for example, the person agrees) |
| APP 7 | Direct marketing | Marketing uses of personal information have their own conditions, including an easy way to opt out and acting on opt-out requests. The marketing rules sit in APP 7, not APP 6 |
| APP 8 | Cross-border disclosure of personal information | Before information goes overseas, take reasonable steps to make sure the overseas recipient will protect it |
| APP 11 | Security of personal information | Take reasonable steps to protect information from misuse, interference, loss and unauthorised access or disclosure, and to destroy or de-identify it when it is no longer needed |
| APP 12 | Access to personal information | Give people access to the information you hold about them when they ask, unless a specific exception applies. If you refuse, give written reasons |
| APP 13 | Correction of personal information | Take reasonable steps to correct information when it is wrong or when the person asks. If you refuse, give written reasons and the ways to complain |

Here is a trap to watch for. If an outsider gets in because protection was weak, think APP 11. If the organisation itself chooses to use or pass on information for a new purpose, think APP 6, or APP 7 if the new purpose is marketing.

Two more things are worth knowing. APP 8 means the organisation stays accountable if an overseas recipient does not protect the information. And since December 2024, the law says the reasonable steps in APP 11 include **technical** measures (such as locks and encryption) and **organisational** measures (such as policies, procedures and staff training).

## When things go wrong: data breaches

A **data breach** is when personal information is lost, or accessed or disclosed without authorisation. The OAIC gives examples such as a lost or stolen device, a hacked database and information sent to the wrong person (OAIC n.d.c).

Under the **Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme**, an organisation or agency covered by the Privacy Act must tell the people affected, and the OAIC, about a breach that is likely to cause **serious harm**. "Likely" means more probable than not. Serious harm can include identity theft, financial loss through fraud, a risk of physical harm, serious psychological harm, or serious damage to a person's reputation. Health details, or several details together, such as a name, birth date and address, make serious harm more likely.

As with the APPs, the scheme is written for APP entities. A council body may be under a different state or territory arrangement for breach notification, so in the challenges you again treat Riverbend as if it were covered.

There are three steps.

1. **Assess.** An organisation that suspects a breach must assess quickly whether serious harm is likely. The OAIC says generally an organisation has 30 days to assess, but quicker is better, because harm can grow while it waits.
2. **Act.** If the organisation quickly fixes the problem so that serious harm is no longer likely, it does not need to notify.
3. **Tell.** If serious harm is likely, the organisation tells the individuals and the OAIC. The notice includes the organisation's name and contact details, a description of the breach, the kinds of information involved, and the steps people should take.

Two more points. If an organisation has sent information overseas under the usual rule and the overseas recipient has a breach, the Australian organisation is still responsible for assessing it and notifying. And when two organisations hold the information, the OAIC suggests that the one with the most direct relationship with the people affected may be best placed to tell them.

This is why a hidden mistake is so costly. If staff are afraid to report a breach, the organisation loses the time it needs to assess and act. That is a legal reason for a no-blame reporting rule, and not only a kindness.

## What changed recently

Parliament passed the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Bill 2024 in late November 2024; it became law in December 2024. The changes most relevant to you are these.

- **A statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy.** A tort is a civil wrong that a person can sue over. It began on 10 June 2025. The OAIC lists two kinds: intruding upon someone's seclusion, and misusing information about them (OAIC 2025). A person can sue another person or an organisation in court. It is not limited to organisations covered by the Privacy Act, and the OAIC does not run these cases.
- **Stronger enforcement.** New tiers of civil penalties, and infringement notices.
- **APP 11 wording.** The technical and organisational measures described above.
- **An overseas list.** A way to prescribe countries and schemes with adequate privacy protection.
- **Automated decisions.** From 10 December 2026, privacy policies must describe the kinds of personal information and decisions involved in certain automated decisions.

For a health clinic, for example, the tort means a person who snoops in records could face a claim as well as the clinic, and the regulator has more ways to act short of going to court.

There is a second law. The *Cyber Security Act 2024* includes minimum standards for smart devices, a Cyber Incident Review Board, and mandatory **ransomware payment reporting**. Ransomware is malicious software that locks data so criminals can demand money. Under the reporting rule, a business that carries on business in Australia, with annual turnover of more than three million dollars, must report a ransomware or cyber extortion payment that it made, or knows was made for it, within 72 hours, using a form on ReportCyber (Australian Signals Directorate n.d.). Responsible entities for critical infrastructure assets must also report a ransomware payment, whatever their turnover. The rules started on 30 May 2025. The law asks for a report after a payment is made. It does not tell anyone whether to pay.

## Challenge: Match ten Riverbend events to the APP (15 minutes)

**The situation.** Treat Riverbend as if it were covered by the APPs, as explained above. For each event, work only from what the event says.

1. A volunteer saves a spreadsheet copy of the member list in an online folder that is set so anyone with the link can open it. A member's neighbour finds the link in a web search and can read members' names, addresses and dates of birth.
2. Riverbend puts the children's session sign-up on an online form. It asks for each child's name, age and allergies and the carer's phone number. The form does not say who runs the sessions, why the details are needed or who may receive them.
3. Members' email addresses were collected to send overdue notices. The library manager now uses them to email promotions for a local business that has paid for the space. Members who reply "STOP" or click "unsubscribe" keep getting the emails.
4. A member asks in writing for a copy of the information the library holds about her. A desk volunteer says, "We don't give those out," and nothing is put in writing. Nothing in the event suggests that handing over the information would put anyone at risk.
5. The new-member form asks every applicant for their employer's name, their past home addresses and a copy of their passport. To issue a library card, the library needs a name, an address and a way to contact the member.
6. The library manager signs up with an online storage company to keep a copy of the member database. The company's servers are in another country. The contract has no privacy terms, and nobody has looked into how the company protects the data.
7. A caller who says he is a member's employer phones the front desk. A volunteer reads out the member's borrowing history, which includes books about challenging a dismissal. The member has not agreed to this.
8. In a cupboard, Riverbend still has paper sign-up sheets for children's sessions from more than ten years ago. They list children's names and allergies. There is no reason to keep them and no process for destroying them.
9. A member emails to ask for a copy of the library's privacy policy. There is no privacy policy to send, nobody knows who deals with privacy questions, and the email is never answered.
10. A carer tells the library twice, once in writing, that her phone number in the children's program record is wrong and asks for it to be corrected. Months later the record still shows the old number, and she has had no reply.

Use the nine APPs in the table above.

1. **Read the table** (3 minutes).
2. **Sort the ten events** (12 minutes). For each event, write the **APP number** it most clearly affects and **one line** saying why. Use only the facts in the event. Each event has one best fit, and two events share an APP.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

One worked answer. Your wording will differ. A reason is sound if it points to a fact in the event.

| # | APP | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **APP 11** | The folder was open to anyone with the link, so an outsider could read the information. This is about keeping information safe |
| 2 | **APP 5** | The form collects personal information but does not say who is collecting it, why, or who may receive it |
| 3 | **APP 7** | The use is direct marketing, and requests to opt out are ignored. Marketing has its own principle |
| 4 | **APP 12** | The member asked for access to the information held about her and was refused with no reason in writing. The event shows no exception |
| 5 | **APP 3** | The form asks for more than is reasonably necessary to issue a library card |
| 6 | **APP 8** | The data goes overseas, and nobody has taken reasonable steps to check that the company will protect it |
| 7 | **APP 6** | Borrowing information collected to lend books was passed to a caller for a different purpose, and the member has not agreed |
| 8 | **APP 11** | Information that is no longer needed is still held, and there is no process to destroy or de-identify it |
| 9 | **APP 1** | There is no privacy policy to provide, and no person or process to deal with privacy questions |
| 10 | **APP 13** | The carer asked for a correction, and the library neither corrected the record nor replied |

**Alternatives and why.** Where two answers fit, a clear reason is accepted. Event 3 is the exception.

- **Event 3:** APP 6 is not accepted. The OAIC's APP 6 guidelines say APP 6 does not apply to use or disclosure for direct marketing, because APP 7 covers it (OAIC n.d.d).
- **Event 10:** APP 10 (quality of personal information) is about keeping information accurate when it is collected, used or disclosed. It is a reasonable second thought, but the carer **asked for a correction**, which is the heart of APP 13.
- **Event 6:** APP 11 could be argued, because the data needs protecting. APP 8 is the closer fit because the key fact is that the data goes overseas with no checks.

If you got four or more wrong, ask: at what stage of the information's life did the problem happen: collecting, using or sharing, keeping safe, or giving back to the person?

### Common mistakes

- **Using APP 11 for every problem.** APP 11 is about keeping information safe and deleting it when it is not needed. If the organisation itself chooses to pass information on for another purpose, that is usually APP 6.
- **Using APP 6 for marketing.** In event 3 the use is plain direct marketing, and the opt-out requests are ignored, so APP 7 is the best fit. Look for the opt-out.
- **Mixing up APP 5 and APP 1.** APP 5 is the notice you give at the time you collect. APP 1 is the privacy policy and the systems behind it.
- **Mixing up APP 3 and APP 5.** APP 3 is about **what** you collect. APP 5 is about **telling people** that you are collecting it.
- **Adding facts the event does not give.** If it does not say what the library did next, do not assume. Use "could" in your reasons where you are guessing.
- **Forgetting that two answers can be the same APP.** Do not change a correct answer only because you used it already.
- **Writing only the number.** The task asks for the number and a reason. The reason is what shows you understand.

## Challenge: Is it a data breach? (10 minutes)

Use events 1 and 7 from the previous challenge, and keep treating Riverbend as if it were covered.

1. **Event 1** (3 minutes). In one or two sentences, say whether it could be a **data breach**, and what Riverbend would need to **assess** next.
2. **Event 7** (3 minutes). Do the same.
3. **The law behind it** (4 minutes). Riverbend is run by a council. In two or three sentences, say why this lesson treats it "as if" the APPs and the NDB scheme applied, and what you would check in real life.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

One worked answer.

1. **Event 1.** It could be a data breach, because information has been accessed without authorisation. Riverbend would need to assess quickly whether serious harm is likely. The files hold names, addresses and dates of birth, and several details together make serious harm more likely. It would also look at who could have seen them. The event does not say how many members are in the file, or whether anyone but the neighbour opened it. Riverbend would also ask whether it can fix the problem fast, for example by closing the link, so that harm is no longer likely.
2. **Event 7.** It could be a data breach, because information has been disclosed without authorisation. Riverbend would need to assess whether serious harm is likely. That means thinking about what the borrowing history could mean for this member, given that it includes books about challenging a dismissal, and what the caller might do with it. The event does not say whether the caller really is the member's employer.
3. **The law behind it.** A council is not a Commonwealth agency, and local councils are generally covered by state or territory privacy law rather than the Privacy Act. The APPs are the national set of principles, so they are a sensible benchmark for practice. In real life I would check which state or territory law applies to the library, what it says about reporting breaches, and who handles privacy questions at the council. The Riverbend page names no one for this, so I would not assume who is responsible until I had checked.

An organisation assesses a suspected breach to decide whether it must notify. Assessing is a separate step from deciding which APP was affected.

### Common mistakes

- **Saying every breach must be notified.** Only a breach that is likely to cause serious harm, and that is not fixed in time, must be notified. The assessment decides.
- **Waiting for 30 days.** Thirty days is generally the limit for finishing the assessment, not a period in which nothing needs to happen.
- **Waiting until you are certain.** "Likely" means more probable than not. Organisations assess a suspected breach.
- **Stating that the APPs bind Riverbend.** A council is generally covered by state or territory law. Say "as if" and name what you would check.
- **Guessing facts the event does not give.** Say "the event does not say".

## Quick check (5 minutes)

1. On 3 March, the owner of a bookshop chain covered by the Privacy Act suspects that someone else has been using a staff member's email account, which holds customer details. She says, "The rules give me 30 days, so I will look into it in April." What is the best advice?

   - A. She is right, because the scheme gives 30 days before the assessment even needs to start.
   - B. She should wait until she is certain that someone has used the customer details.
   - C. She should notify the customers at once and skip the assessment, to be on the safe side.
   - D. She should assess promptly, because 30 days is generally the limit, and harm may grow.

2. A two-person speech pathology practice has an annual turnover well under three million dollars. The owner says the Privacy Act does not apply, because the practice is a small business. Is the owner right?

   - A. Yes, because only businesses with a turnover over three million dollars are covered.
   - B. No, because a private health service provider that holds health information is covered even if it is small.
   - C. Yes, because the Act covers only hospitals and not private practices.
   - D. No, but only if the practice has more than one staff member.

3. An Australian furniture maker with annual turnover of twelve million dollars pays a ransom to get its files back. What must it do?

   - A. Report the payment within 72 hours using the ransomware payment form.
   - B. Report only if the files were not returned.
   - C. Report within 72 hours of receiving the demand, even if it did not pay.
   - D. Nothing, because only critical infrastructure must report.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

1. **D.** Thirty days is generally the limit for assessing, and harm can grow while an organisation waits, so quicker is better. Option A treats the 30 days as a waiting period, and option B waits for certainty when the test is "likely". Option C skips the assessment, which is the step that decides whether notifying is needed.
2. **B.** A private health service provider that holds health information is covered by the Privacy Act, whatever its size. Option A forgets this exception, option C is wrong about who is covered, and option D invents a staffing test.
3. **A.** The rule applies after a payment is made by a business over the turnover threshold. The report is about the payment, so option C is wrong, and option B adds a condition that is not in the rule. Option D is wrong because the rule is not limited to critical infrastructure.

## Reflect (5 minutes)

Choose one prompt and write 3 to 6 sentences. Do not name other people or share private details such as passwords, card numbers or account details.

- **Protecting yourself with encryption.** Can you use encryption to protect yourself? Think about what you do already (for example a screen lock, a messaging app or a secure website) and what encryption cannot do. What is one thing you could start doing, or stop doing, to protect your own information?
- **Owning up quickly.** The breach scheme works best when mistakes are reported fast. What would make you report a mistake at work, such as a message sent to the wrong person, within the hour? What would stop you?

**Likely misconceptions.**

- Learners use APP 11 for everything, or APP 6 for marketing. Ask "what stage of the information's life?" and "is there an opt-out?".

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: keypoints

- The Privacy Act and its 13 APPs bind most Australian Government agencies and some private organisations. Many small businesses are outside it, but a private health service provider that holds health information is covered whatever its size.
- A council-run body is generally covered by state or territory privacy law instead. Find out which law applies before you say what an organisation must do.
- To pick the APP, ask what stage of the information's life the problem happened at. Outsiders getting in is usually APP 11. The organisation choosing a new use is usually APP 6, or APP 7 for marketing.
- For a suspected breach: assess quickly, act to reduce harm, and tell the people affected and the OAIC if serious harm is likely.
- The 2024 changes added a right to sue for serious invasions of privacy, stronger enforcement and APP 11 wording on technical and organisational measures. The *Cyber Security Act 2024* requires some businesses to report ransomware payments within 72 hours.
