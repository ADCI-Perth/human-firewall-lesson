## Your digital life

Think about yesterday. Your phone woke you up. You may have checked messages before getting out of bed, tapped a card or a watch to pay for coffee, caught a bus with a travel card, or logged in to a learning site. Perhaps you booked a doctor's appointment online or ordered dinner through an app.

Every one of those steps sent information somewhere: your name, your location, your payment details, the time. It travelled across networks to companies you may never think about. Most of the time that is fine, and it is why life is so convenient. The point is to notice how much of your life now sits on other people's systems.

McAfee and Brynjolfsson (2018) argue that three changes are happening together: smarter machines doing work that people used to do, online platforms where many people and businesses meet, and the crowd of people who contribute information and ideas online. You do not need the whole book to use their main point. Digital technology now shapes how people, groups and organisations work, so a weakness in it can reach all three.

## Three levels

We will look at the effect of digital networks at three levels. You will use them again and again in this course.

| Level | What networks gave | What networks cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Individuals** | Speed and convenience. You can bank at midnight, in your pyjamas. | You hand over more personal information than people did a generation ago, to more organisations, and you often cannot see where it ends up. |
| **Groups**, such as families, sports clubs and friendship circles | Easy contact. Everyone stays in touch. | A single careless post can reveal things about everybody in the group. |
| **Organisations** | They can serve thousands of customers at once. | They now depend on those networks. If systems go down or data leaks, the business can stop and customers lose trust. |

## Why do we protect data and systems?

Security people usually give three reasons. You can think of them as three promises.

- **Confidentiality:** information is seen only by the people who should see it.
- **Integrity:** information is accurate and has not been changed by someone who should not change it.
- **Availability:** the information and systems are there when you need them.

Break any one promise and real people are affected. A leaked medical record breaks confidentiality. A bank balance altered by a criminal breaks integrity. A hospital that cannot open its patient files breaks availability.

## Why attackers go for people

Imagine a house with a strong front door, three locks and an alarm, and an owner who will open the door for anyone who sounds confident and in a hurry. A burglar would not bother with the locks.

Attackers have noticed that the easiest way into a digital system is often not the system at all. It is the person using it. Tricking people into giving up information, access or money is called **social engineering**, and the next episode covers it in detail. For now, hold on to this idea: technology protects data, but people use the technology, so people are part of the defence.

## What it costs when an attack works

We use the same three levels to look at the harm.

- **Individuals.** Money lost, accounts taken over, and stress. *Identity theft* is when someone uses your personal details, such as your name and date of birth, to pretend to be you. Fixing it can take months. The harm is not only financial. People often feel embarrassed, and embarrassment stops them from reporting.
- **Groups.** One person's compromised account can be used to attack their contacts, because a message from a friend or colleague is trusted. A group's trust in each other can also be damaged.
- **Organisations.** Money, lost work time, legal and regulatory action, and a damaged reputation. Customers whose information is exposed often become targets for further scams, because criminals can use real details to sound convincing.

These levels connect. An individual falls for a message, that gives the attacker access to the organisation, and the organisation's customers pay the price. When you analyse an incident, separate the people hurt first from the people hurt later.

## Challenge: Follow the harm (5 minutes)

A fictional scenario. Priya works at a small physiotherapy clinic. An email arrives that looks as if it is from the clinic owner. It says: "I'm in a meeting. Buy five gift cards right now and send me the codes. I'll explain later." Priya buys the cards with her own money and sends the codes. The email was from an attacker.

Write one or two sentences for each level: **individual**, **group** and **organisation**. For each, say who is hurt, how, and whether they are hurt first or later. Then name one promise (confidentiality, integrity or availability) that might also be broken if the attacker went further.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

One worked answer. Yours may differ.

- **Individual:** Priya loses her own money, and she may feel embarrassed and delay telling anyone. She is hurt first.
- **Group:** if the attacker reached her mailbox, her colleagues could be targeted next. They are hurt later.
- **Organisation:** the clinic loses time dealing with the incident, and its owner's name has been used to deceive staff. Patients could be hurt later if the attacker used what they learned for more scams.
- **Promise:** if the attacker gained access to patient emails, confidentiality would be broken. If they changed appointment records, integrity would be.

## Challenge: How has one everyday task changed in 50 years? (20 minutes)

Digital networks are part of almost every everyday task. When a task moves online, three things change: who you deal with, what information you hand over, and where that information goes.

Pick **one everyday task** and compare how it was done about 50 years ago with how it is done now. Choose one of these, or pick your own:

- banking (paying a bill or checking your balance)
- booking a doctor's appointment
- finding a job
- buying a train or bus ticket

1. **Choose your task** (1 minute).
2. **Describe "then"** (3 minutes). Use the notes below. You do not need exact dates. As an optional extra, ask an older relative or neighbour how they did it.
3. **Describe "now"** (3 minutes). Think about the last time you did the task yourself and write down each step.
4. **Fill in a table** (8 minutes) with these three rows and two columns, "Then" and "Now". Use one or two short sentences in each cell.

   | | Then (about 50 years ago) | Now |
   |---|---|---|
   | Who did you deal with? | | |
   | What information did you hand over? | | |
   | Where did that information go? | | |

5. **Write 3 to 4 sentences** (5 minutes) answering: *What got easier? What new risk appeared, and who could take advantage of it?*

Do not use anyone's real name or private details. Describe your own experience, or a general experience of "an older person".

**Notes on "then".** These describe the usual way, not exact dates.

| Task | How it was usually done about 50 years ago |
|---|---|
| Banking | Visit a branch in business hours with a passbook or cheque book. A teller recorded your transaction by hand or on a branch machine. Paper statements came by post. |
| Booking a doctor | Phone or visit the surgery. A receptionist wrote it in an appointment book. Your records were kept on paper in the surgery. |
| Finding a job | Read newspaper advertisements, ask people you knew, or visit an employment office. You posted or handed in a paper application. |
| Buying a ticket | Queue at the station window and pay in cash. A staff member issued a paper ticket. |

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

This worked answer uses booking a doctor's appointment. Your task may be different. Check that your answers have the same kind of detail.

| | Then (about 50 years ago) | Now |
|---|---|---|
| Who did you deal with? | Usually one person: the receptionist, by phone or in person at the surgery. | The receptionist, but also an online booking company or app, the clinic's software supplier, and possibly a text-message service. |
| What information did you hand over? | Your name, a phone number or address, and a short reason. Most of it was spoken or written on paper. | Your name, date of birth, mobile number, email and Medicare number. You may also tick the reason for the visit and agree to terms you did not read in full. |
| Where did that information go? | A paper diary and a filing cabinet at the clinic, kept in one building. | A company's database, possibly in a cloud service in another place or country, shared across several systems, and sometimes copied to your phone's apps. |

**Short answer:** Booking is now faster and I can do it at any hour without queuing. But more companies now hold my details, and I cannot see where they are stored. An attacker no longer needs to break into one filing cabinet. They only need to trick one person or one company, for example by sending a fake text that looks like a booking reminder and asks me to "confirm my details". Because the information is held in many places, a problem with any one of them can affect me.

### Common mistakes

- **Comparing technology instead of the task.** "Then there were no phones" does not answer the question. Describe who you dealt with and what you handed over.
- **Leaving out where the information goes.** "It's online" is not an answer. Say where it is kept and who else can see it.
- **Only listing good things.** Say what got easier, but also name one new risk.
- **Guessing exact dates or numbers.** Use "usually" and "typically". Do not invent facts.
- **Naming real people.** Keep family stories general, with no names or private details.

## Quick check (5 minutes)

1. Dr Okafor's clinic is hit by an attack, and for two days staff cannot open any patient files. Which of the three promises of security has been broken?

   - A. Confidentiality
   - B. Integrity
   - C. Availability
   - D. None. No information was leaked, so no promise was broken.

2. Mia posts a photo from her netball team's trip, with the players' names and the hotel's name in the caption. Which level of impact does this show most clearly?

   - A. Individual only, because Mia took the photo.
   - B. Group, because one post can reveal things about everybody in the group.
   - C. Organisation, because netball clubs are businesses.
   - D. None, because the photo is not private.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

1. **C.** Availability means information and systems are there when you need them. Nothing was leaked or changed, but staff could not use the files, and patients are affected. Option D is wrong because availability is one of the three promises, even when nothing is stolen.
2. **B.** A single careless post can reveal things about everyone in the group, such as who is travelling together and where they are staying. Option A misses the other players, option C confuses a club with a business, and option D forgets that a public caption can be read by anyone.

## Reflect (5 minutes)

Choose one or two prompts and write 3 to 6 sentences for each. Do not name other people or share private details such as passwords.

- **Your digital life.** How much of your own day depends on digital networks? Think of one ordinary day and list the apps, devices and online services you used. How do you think life was different for people 50 years ago?
- **If it all failed.** Imagine the networks you rely on stopped working for a week. What would happen in your life and in your community? Who might want to make a network fail, and why? Use only well-documented real examples, not rumours.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: instructor

**Timing (55 minutes).** Teaching text 20 minutes (read aloud in sections, or ask learners to read it first). Exercises 35 minutes: Follow the harm 5, then-and-now challenge 20, quick check 5, discussion 5. The optional interview with a relative is homework, not class time.

**Likely misconceptions.**

- Learners compare technology ("no phones then") instead of the task. Ask "who did you deal with?" to pull them back.
- Learners think security only means keeping secrets. Use the hospital example to show that availability and integrity matter as much as confidentiality.
- Learners assume attackers need technical skill. Leave the answer for episode 2, but plant the question here.

**Running the discussion.** Use think-pair-share (see the general notes). The "if it all failed" prompt works well with a short whole-room list of what each pair would lose first.

**Tip.** Collect the "new risks" from the then-and-now challenge on a board.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: keypoints

- Digital networks bring speed and convenience, and they also spread our information across more organisations than before.
- Look at impact on three levels: individuals, groups and organisations.
- We protect data and systems to keep three promises: confidentiality, integrity and availability.
- Attackers often target the person using a system, because it is easier than breaking the technology.
- The harm from an attack spreads, so separate the people hurt first from the people hurt later.
