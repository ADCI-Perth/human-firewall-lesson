## Three things, and each has a limit

A good plan for the people side of security rests on three things: what staff know, who is let in, and how much any one person can reach. Each has a limit.

- **Awareness.** Staff cannot follow rules they have never heard, or rules that are too vague to use. This episode covers it.
- **Vetting.** Checking a person before you give them trust tells you about their past, and nothing about their future. The next episode covers it.
- **Access.** The more one person can reach, the more damage one mistake or one bad decision can do. The next episode covers this too.

A good plan uses all three together. We start with awareness.

## Awareness is not the same as a policy

**Awareness** means that staff understand what the risks are and what is expected of them. It is not the same as having a policy. A policy can sit in a folder that nobody opens. Awareness shows up in what people do on a busy Tuesday.

Why does it matter so much? Most people who break a rule are not careless on purpose. They are rushing, or they never knew the rule, or the safe way felt slower and nobody explained why it mattered. Awareness closes that gap. It tells people what the risk is, in plain words, and what you want them to do instead.

## Five things that make awareness work

| Feature | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Short** | Ten or twenty minutes that people take in beat a two-hour session they sit through. |
| **Specific** | Use examples from your own workplace, not a general warning about hackers. |
| **Role-based** | A receptionist, an accountant and a manager meet different risks, so each group needs different examples. |
| **Repeated** | Starting a new job is the best moment to teach. A short reminder every few months stops it fading. Part-timers, casuals and contractors need it too, because attackers do not check someone's hours first. |
| **Safe to report** | If people fear blame, they hide mistakes. A hidden mistake is a much bigger problem than an admitted one. |

## How will you know it worked?

Counting how many people attended a session only tells you that they sat in a room. Better evidence is about behaviour.

- Are more suspicious messages being reported?
- Do spot checks find fewer devices left unlocked?
- Do fewer people click on a test phishing message?

Notice that **more reports can be a good sign**. It can mean people feel safe to speak up, not that more attacks are happening.

## Your own ICT life

The habits you have at home come with you to work. A phone with no passcode, an old account you forgot, or one password kept in a plain note can become a workplace problem. It is also easier to ask other people to change a habit once you have changed one of your own, because you know how it feels.

The first challenge asks you to check your own ICT security. It is private.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: challenge

## Challenge: Check your own ICT security (15 minutes)

**This activity is private.** Keep your answers to yourself. Nobody needs to read your list.

- **Never write down, type, photograph or share a password, a PIN, a recovery code or an account number.** Not in the checklist, and not with anyone else.
- **Check only your own devices, accounts and home network.** Do not check anyone else's phone or laptop, and do not try to log in to a router, device or account that is not yours.
- If you share a device with family, check only the parts that belong to you.

1. **Make a list** (3 minutes). On paper or in a note on your own device, list what you use in five areas: **devices** (phones, tablets, laptops, a smart TV, a game console, and any old ones in a drawer), **apps and accounts** (email, banking, social media, cloud storage, shopping), **password storage** (where your passwords live), **networks** (home Wi-Fi, mobile data, public Wi-Fi you use) and **providers** (your email, phone, internet and cloud storage companies).
2. **Go through the checklist** (7 minutes). For each line write **Yes**, **Partly**, **No** or **Not applicable**. Menus differ between devices. If you cannot find a setting in a couple of minutes, write "unsure" and move on.
3. **Choose one change** (3 minutes). Look at your **No** and **Partly** answers. Pick **one** that is small enough to do this week. Write it as a specific action with a when, for example "Turn on automatic updates on my laptop tonight".
4. **Compare** (2 minutes). Open the worked answer and compare how it picks a change.

**Devices**

1. Every device I use has a screen lock (passcode, fingerprint or face).
2. My devices install updates, and none of them is so old that it no longer receives updates from its maker.
3. If my phone or laptop were lost, I could find it or erase it remotely, and I have turned that feature on.
4. I have a recent copy of the files and photos I could not replace, stored somewhere that is not always connected to the device.
5. Before I sell, give away or recycle a device, I know how to reset it and sign out of my accounts.

**Apps and accounts**

6. I have used each app and account in the past year, or I have closed it.
7. My email account, which can reset my other accounts, has two-step verification turned on.
8. My banking and any cloud storage have two-step verification turned on.
9. I install apps only from the official store for my device, and I have checked the permissions the apps ask for.
10. I know which accounts have my payment card saved.

**Password storage**

11. My passwords are kept in a password manager or another protected place, and not in a plain note or a notes app.
12. Each important account has its own password.
13. I know what I would do if I thought a password had been stolen.

**Networks**

14. My home Wi-Fi uses a password that I set, and not the one printed on the router.
15. My router's own settings are protected by a password that I set (if you have never looked, write "unsure").
16. When I use public Wi-Fi, I avoid banking and other sensitive tasks, or I use mobile data instead.
17. Guests at home use a guest network or a separate password, if my router offers one.

**Providers**

18. I know which company holds my email, my photos and my backups.
19. I have looked at what at least one of these providers says it does with my information, and what it lets me control.
20. I know how to contact each provider, or report a problem, if my account is hacked.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Worked answer

Your results are private, so there is no "right" set of answers. This worked answer shows what a good result and a good change look like. It is a **fictional learner's** check, and part of the list only. Yours will be different.

| # | Result | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Yes | Phone and laptop are locked. The old tablet in the drawer has no passcode. |
| 2 | Partly | Laptop and phone update automatically. The old tablet has not been updated for years. |
| 4 | No | Photos are on the phone only. |
| 5 | Unsure | Has never reset a device. |
| 6 | No | Found about a dozen old accounts, including a forum from school. |
| 7 | Yes | Email has two-step verification. |
| 11 | Partly | Most passwords are in a password manager, and a few are in a phone note. |
| 14 | Yes | Changed the router password when it was installed. |
| 19 | No | Has never read what the cloud photo service does with photos. |

**What a good change looks like.** The learner has several gaps. They did not try to fix everything. They picked the gap that protects the most important thing and is small enough to do:

> *My change: tonight I will move the passwords in my phone note into my password manager and delete the note.*

**Why this is a good choice.** It is **specific** (which passwords, and where they go). It has a **when**. It takes **well under an hour**. And it deals with a gap that could cause real harm. Other good changes from this list would be to turn on backups for the photos (item 4) or to close the old forum account (item 6). Any change is fine if it is real, specific and can be done this week.

**Not so good changes.** "Be more careful online" is not specific. "Change all my passwords" is too big, and has no plan. "Buy an antivirus program" is a product, not a habit, and it does not deal with the gaps found.

**Checking yourself.** You checked your own things only. You answered each line, or wrote "unsure". Your change is one action with a when, and it contains no password or number. If you found nothing to change, pick a line you wrote "unsure" for and find out.

### Common mistakes

- **Sharing the checklist or a password.** Keep your results to yourself. Never write a password, PIN or account number anywhere in this activity.
- **Choosing a change that is too big.** "Fix everything" does not get done. Pick one action for this week.
- **Choosing a vague change.** "Be safer" cannot be checked. Say what you will do and when.
- **Skipping the old things.** The tablet in the drawer and the account from years ago are part of your ICT life too.
- **Feeling bad about the gaps.** Almost everyone has some. The point is to make one change.
- **Checking other people's devices or home network.** This activity is only about your own.
- **Giving up because you could not find a setting.** Write "unsure" and move on. Your device maker's help pages show how.

## Challenge: Fix the awareness (10 minutes)

A fictional scenario. A removalist firm emails a long security policy to everyone once a year. Months later, drivers still leave job sheets showing customers' addresses on the seats of unlocked vans.

Write a short plan to change what the drivers do. Use the five features from this episode: **short**, **specific**, **role-based**, **repeated** and **safe to report**. Your plan should say:

1. who it is for and how long it takes,
2. what example it uses,
3. how often it is repeated,
4. how a driver reports a slip, and
5. how the firm will know that behaviour changed.

Two to four lines for each point is enough. The scenario does not say who runs awareness at the firm, so name a role and say why you chose it.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Worked answer

One worked answer. Yours may differ, and it can be sound with different details.

1. **Who and how long.** Drivers, and anyone who rides with them. A 10-minute session at the start of a shift, not a document to read alone.
2. **Example.** The firm's own job sheet, held up in the session. Show what a stranger could learn from a sheet left on a seat: a name and an address. Then show the safe habit: keep sheets in a folder, take them with you, and lock the van.
3. **Repetition.** In a new driver's first week, then a 5-minute reminder at the depot every three months. Casual and part-time drivers get the same session.
4. **Reporting.** A simple way to say "I left a sheet in the van", for example a text to the depot supervisor. The firm says clearly that a reported slip is not punished, so drivers do not hide it.
5. **Checking.** Not the number who attended. Instead, spot checks of parked vans for sheets in view, before and after, and the number of slips reported. If reports go up at first, that can be a good sign.

**Owner.** The depot supervisor, because they see the drivers daily and can run a short session and do spot checks. The scenario does not say who this is at the firm, so this is a choice, and you could choose another role.

**Why the email did not work.** It was long, general, sent to everyone, sent once a year, and gave no way to report. It fails all five features.

## Quick check (5 minutes)

1. A veterinary practice runs a 15-minute session on spotting fake supplier emails. Three months later, the manager wants evidence that staff behaviour has changed. Which measure is best?

   - A. The number of staff who attended the session, taken from the sign-in sheet.
   - B. How highly staff rated the session on the feedback form handed out straight afterwards.
   - C. Whether staff report more suspicious emails, and fewer click on risky ones.
   - D. Scores on a quiz that staff took on the day of the session, while it was fresh.

2. Last year a firm named, in front of the whole team, the staff who clicked on a test phishing email. Now Lena has clicked on a real suspicious link. She is worried and thinks she should say nothing and hope it is fine. What would make her most likely to report it?

   - A. A reminder that the policy says all incidents must be reported.
   - B. A warning that anyone who hides a mistake will be disciplined.
   - C. A promise that reports are handled privately and without blame, and a simple way to make one.
   - D. A longer session on how phishing works.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

1. **C.** Reports and risky clicks are behaviour. More reports can be a good sign, because it means staff feel safe to speak up. Option A shows only that people were in the room. Option B shows that people enjoyed the session, not that their habits changed. Option D shows what people remember on the day, not what they do weeks later.
2. **C.** People hide mistakes when they fear blame, and naming staff in public teaches exactly that. A promise of private, no-blame handling and an easy way to report deals with the real barrier. Options A and B repeat the rule or add a threat, which makes hiding more likely. Option D builds knowledge, but Lena already knows she clicked. Her problem is fear, not knowledge.

## Reflect (5 minutes)

Write 3 to 6 sentences. This reflection is private to you. If you do this in a group, you choose what, if anything, to share with a partner. Describe your habits in general terms. Do not share passwords, answers to security questions, card numbers, account details or your address.

- **Your own ICT life.** How secure is your personal ICT life? Think about the devices, social media, apps, password storage, network connections and service providers you use. Where are they, and how secure do you think each one is? Which one worries you most, and what was the one change you chose in the first challenge?

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: instructor

**Timing (55 minutes).** Teaching text 20 minutes. Exercises 35 minutes: personal ICT check 15, fix the awareness 10, quick check 5, discussion 5.

**Keep the personal check private.** Do not ask anyone to read out, show or hand in their checklist, and do not walk around looking at screens. Remind the room that the worked answer is a fictional learner and guidance, not a set of correct personal answers. If you ask for anything at the end, ask only "who has picked a change?" and let people say as much or as little as they like. Remind everyone not to check anyone else's devices or the venue's network or router.

**Likely misconceptions.**

- Learners think awareness means a policy or a signed form. Ask what a driver or a receptionist did differently on Tuesday.
- Learners measure attendance or satisfaction. Ask "what would you see if it worked?"
- Learners choose an enormous change in the personal check. Ask "can you do that tonight?"
- Learners feel ashamed of their gaps. Say that almost everyone has some.

**Running the discussion.** Use think-pair-share (see the general notes), but keep the pair stage general: "what kind of gap did you find?" and not "what is your password habit?" If anyone would rather not talk, let them write only.

**Tip.** For the fix-the-awareness challenge, ask pairs to swap plans and check them against the five features, ticking each one.
