## Glossary

Plain definitions of the terms used in this lesson, in alphabetical order. Where a reading uses a slightly different word, match the idea, not the label.

### A

Access management
: The half of identity and access management about what each identity can do. It has two steps: authentication, then authorisation.

Account takeover
: When someone else gains control of a real account, so posts from it may look as if the owner wrote them. Also called a hacked account.

Algorithm
: A set of rules a computer follows to decide something, such as which posts you see.

APP (Australian Privacy Principle)
: One of the 13 principles in the Privacy Act that set how organisations must collect, use, keep and share personal information.

APP entity
: An organisation or agency that must follow the Australian Privacy Principles. A private health service provider holding health information is covered even if it is small.

Attributes
: Facts about you that identify you, such as your name, date of birth and address. They are hard to change.

Authentication
: Checking that a person is who they say they are, for example with a password, a phone code or a fingerprint.

Authorisation
: Deciding what an authenticated person is allowed to open or do.

Availability
: The promise that information and systems are there when you need them.

### B

Baiting
: A trick that offers something tempting, such as a USB drive labelled "Wages", so that curiosity leads you to plug in or open it.

Big data
: Very large collections of information that are analysed to find patterns, which can reveal things you never shared.

Bin raiding
: Searching rubbish or recycling for papers that show names, account numbers or passwords. Shredding is the defence.

BYOD (bring your own device)
: A policy that lets staff use their own phones or laptops for work. It lowers cost but mixes personal and work data.

### C

Compartmentalisation
: Limiting each person's access to systems, and their knowledge, to what their job needs, so one failure cannot spread everywhere.

Compromised insider
: An insider whose account or device an outsider has taken over. The login is theirs, but someone else is using it.

Confidentiality
: The promise that information is seen only by the people who should see it.

Credentials
: What you use to prove you are you, such as a password, a code or a fingerprint. They can be replaced.

Cyber espionage
: Spying done through computer systems, for example by hacking or phishing. It is one method of espionage, not a separate kind.

Cyber war
: Attacks on systems by or between countries. This is a plain definition used in this lesson. Experts disagree about when something counts as war.

Cybercrime
: Crime that uses computers or networks, or targets them.

Cyberterrorism
: Attacks on computer systems meant to cause fear or serious harm for a political or ideological goal. This is a plain definition used in this lesson.

### D

Data breach
: When personal information is accessed or disclosed without authorisation, or is lost, for example a lost device, a hacked database or an email sent to the wrong person.

Digital footprint
: The trail of information about you that exists online, including what you post and what other people post about you.

Digital network
: Computers, phones and other devices connected so they can share information.

Doxxing
: Collecting a person's private details, such as a home address or workplace, and publishing them so others can find them.

### E

Economic espionage
: Secretly taking trade secrets or other valuable information to benefit a foreign state.

Encryption
: Scrambling information so that only someone with the right key can read it. It protects information if a device is lost or a message is intercepted.

Espionage
: Secretly getting information that someone wants to keep private. Corporate espionage is done for a business, to get a competitor's designs, prices or plans. State espionage is done for a government, to get political, military or economic secrets.

### H

Harvard referencing
: A referencing style that cites the author and year in the text and lists the full details of every source at the end.

### I

Identity and access management (IAM)
: The rules, processes and tools that make sure the right people have the right access, to the right things, at the right time, and nobody else.

Identity lifecycle
: The stages an identity goes through: created when a person joins, updated when their job changes, and ended when they leave.

Identity theft
: Using another person's personal details to pretend to be them, usually to get money, goods or services.

Impersonation
: Someone posting or acting as you to damage your name or fool other people.

Industrial espionage
: Secretly taking confidential business information, such as designs, prices or client lists, to benefit a private business, such as a competitor. It often overlaps with economic espionage.

Information flow
: The route personal information takes through an organisation: collected, stored, used and shared. Mark where it crosses a boundary.

Insider threat
: A risk that comes from someone with legitimate access to an organisation's systems, buildings or information.

Integrity
: The promise that information is accurate and has not been changed by someone who should not change it.

Internet of Things (IoT)
: Everyday objects, such as watches, doorbells and baby monitors, that connect to the internet. Each is another device to update.

### L

Layered defence
: Using several different protections together, so if one fails another still helps. Also called a multi-layered approach.

Least privilege
: Giving each person only the access they need to do their job, and no more.

Legitimate access
: Access that a person has been properly given, for example through their job.

### M

Malicious insider
: An insider who causes harm on purpose, for reasons such as money or revenge.

Mitigation
: An action that reduces the chance or the harm of something bad happening.

Multi-factor authentication (MFA)
: Proving who you are with two or more different kinds of proof. A password plus a PIN does not count, because both are things you know.

### N

Need to know
: The idea that a person should get only the information their work requires.

Negligent insider
: An insider who causes harm by accident, usually by taking a shortcut or making a mistake, without meaning any harm.

Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme
: The rule that an organisation covered by the Privacy Act must tell the people affected, and the OAIC, about a breach likely to cause serious harm.

### O

OAIC
: The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner, Australia's national privacy regulator.

Ongoing assessment
: Keeping a person's suitability for trust and access under review after the first checks, not only at hiring.

### P

Personal information
: Information about a person who is identified, or who could reasonably be identified.

Phishing
: A fake message, usually an email, that looks as if it comes from someone you trust, to get you to click, open or hand over something.

Phishing simulation
: A test in which an organisation sends its own staff fake phishing messages to see who responds.

Pretexting
: Inventing a believable story, the pretext, to get information. The story is the whole attack.

Privacy impact assessment (PIA)
: A structured review of a project that finds how it might affect people's privacy and recommends ways to manage, reduce or remove those effects.

Provisioning and de-provisioning
: Giving a person the accounts and access they need, and removing them when they no longer need them.

### Q

Quantum computing
: A very different kind of computer that experts warn could one day break some of today's encryption.

Quid pro quo
: Latin for something for something. The attacker offers a favour or service, such as free tech support, in return for information or access.

### R

Ransomware
: Malicious software that locks data so criminals can demand money.

Risk assessment
: Working out what could go wrong, how likely it is and how bad it would be.

### S

Sensitive information
: A more protected kind of personal information. Health information is one example.

Serious harm
: Harm such as identity theft, financial loss through fraud, serious psychological harm, a risk of physical harm, or serious damage to reputation.

Shoulder surfing
: Watching someone type a password or PIN, or reading their screen, over their shoulder.

Single sign-on
: Logging in once to reach several systems. It is convenient, but a stolen login opens them all.

Smishing and vishing
: Smishing is scam messages by text. Vishing is scam phone calls.

Social engineering
: Tricking people into giving up information, access or money by using their trust, habits and emotions instead of attacking the technology.

Spear phishing
: Phishing aimed at a particular person or organisation, using real details about them to seem convincing.

Stakeholders
: The people and groups affected by a project or who have a say in it, such as customers, staff and partners.

Statutory tort
: A civil wrong created by an Act of Parliament, which a person can sue over. It covers serious invasions of privacy.

### T

Tailgating
: Following an authorised person through a secure door, often because they politely held it open. Also called piggybacking.

Technical controls
: Protections built into technology, such as passwords, access limits and activity logs. They work best alongside people and culture controls.

Threshold assessment
: A quick written first check that decides whether a project needs a full PIA.

Trade secret
: Valuable business information that is kept confidential, such as a design, a recipe or a client list.

Two-person approval
: Requiring a second person to approve high-risk actions, such as large payments, so one person cannot act alone.

Two-step verification (2SV)
: A second check when you log in, such as a code sent to your phone. It is the same idea as multi-factor authentication.

### U

Under-reporting
: When victims do not report incidents, for example because they do not know they were targeted or fear for their reputation.

### V

Vetting
: Checking a person before giving them trust and access. It is a snapshot and only one layer of defence.

### W

Walk-up access
: Using a computer or phone that someone left unlocked or unattended.
