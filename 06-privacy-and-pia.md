## What is privacy?

Privacy has several meanings. In this course we mean **information privacy**: your ability to know what personal information is collected about you, how it is used and who it is shared with, and to have a say.

**Personal information** is anything that identifies you or could reasonably identify you. Some of it is **sensitive**, such as health information, and needs more care.

## Privacy harms

Privacy is not just a feeling. When it fails, real harms follow.

- **Money lost** through fraud.
- **Identity stolen**, as you saw in [Identity, identity management and identity theft](03-identity.md).
- **Embarrassment or unfair treatment**, if private information such as a health condition becomes known.
- **Risk of being found** by someone dangerous.
- **Lost trust and control**, because you cannot take information back once it is out.

Harms spread, using the three levels from [Digital lives](01-digital-lives.md). One person's exposed health information is an individual harm. A family or team who now doubt each other's messages is a group harm. An organisation facing complaints, costs and lost trust is an organisational harm. Often one leads to the next.

## Who is responsible?

Organisations that collect your information carry most of the duty. But you carry some too.

- Think before you post.
- Share only what a service needs. A running app does not need your contacts or photos.
- Check your settings.
- At work, treat other people's information with the care you would want for your own.

## An ethical response and a practical response

When privacy goes wrong, how should an organisation respond? Think about two things.

- An **ethical response** asks who is affected and how, and what would be fair to them. It usually means being honest, reducing harm and putting people's interests alongside the organisation's.
- A **practical response** asks what the organisation can actually do, who will do it, by when, and how it will check that it worked. A tiny charity cannot copy what a bank does.

The best answer is both, and the two should not contradict each other. It also helps to compare two options. Suppose a business leaks customer emails. It could quietly fix the fault and say nothing, or tell customers and explain how to protect themselves. Telling people costs more at first, but it respects them and lets them act. Say which you would choose, and why. You do not need to invent costs. Say who does the work, when, and why it suits the size of the organisation.

## Privacy impact assessments

A **privacy impact assessment (PIA)** is a structured review of a project. It asks how the project could affect people's privacy, and how to avoid or reduce those effects. The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner, or OAIC, publishes a free guide (OAIC 2021). Doing a PIA early helps a project meet the law, saves the cost of fixing mistakes later, and shows people that their privacy was considered.

### The threshold assessment

Not every project needs the same depth. So the OAIC's first step is a **threshold assessment**: a quick check, usually short, that you write down. It starts with a simple question. Will any personal information be collected, stored, used or disclosed? If not, a PIA is unlikely to be needed.

If the answer is yes, you look at how sensitive the information is, what it will be used for, what is changing in how information is handled, and what the people affected might think. You then record whether you will do a full PIA, and who made that decision.

The OAIC strongly encourages organisations to do PIAs for projects that involve personal information. The legal duty to do one for high privacy risk projects applies to Australian Government agencies, but the idea suits any organisation.

### The ten steps

If the answer is yes, the guide sets out ten steps:

1. Threshold assessment
2. Plan the PIA
3. Describe the project
4. Identify and consult stakeholders
5. Map information flows
6. Analyse the privacy impacts and check compliance
7. Manage the risks
8. Make recommendations
9. Write a report
10. Respond and review

Two are worth a note now. **Mapping information flows** means tracing where information comes from, where it is kept and who receives it. **Recommendations** should say who should act and by when. The OAIC guide in Go deeper covers all ten steps.

## Challenge: Does Riverbend's new app need a full PIA? (20 minutes)

Before an organisation starts a big privacy review, it makes a threshold assessment. Here you do that check for a project at Riverbend.

**The project.** The library manager has proposed a new Riverbend member app. It would be built and hosted by an outside software company. Members could log in to see what they have borrowed and renew books. The app would suggest books based on each member's borrowing history. Carers could also sign their children up for the weekend children's sessions in the app, entering the same details the library holds now: the child's name, age, carer contact details, allergies and photo consent. The proposal has not been approved yet.

Use the [Riverbend page](../learners/riverbend.md) for the facts about the library. If you need a detail that is not there, say what you are assuming.

**The threshold questions.** The OAIC guide sets out a threshold assessment, but not as a numbered list. These six questions put its points in our own words. Questions 1 to 5 follow the guide. Question 6 is added by this lesson.

| # | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Personal information:** will the project collect, store, use or share information about identifiable people? What kinds? |
| 2 | **Sensitivity:** is any of it especially sensitive, such as health information? Are the people involved children or others who may need extra care? |
| 3 | **Purposes:** what is the information being collected and used for? |
| 4 | **Change:** is something new about how the information is collected, stored, used or shared (for example new technology or a new organisation involved)? |
| 5 | **Stakeholders:** who is affected, and does the scenario show that their views have been asked for? |
| 6 | **Scale and handling (added by this lesson):** roughly how many people are affected, and who else will handle the information? |

1. **Read the Riverbend page** (3 minutes).
2. **Answer the six questions** (10 minutes). For each, write one or two sentences and point to a detail in the scenario.
3. **Make the decision** (7 minutes). Write:
   - one of: **"Full PIA needed"**, **"No full PIA needed"** or **"Not sure"**;
   - **two or three reasons**, using your answers above;
   - **who** should be responsible for the PIA at the library and **when** you would suggest it starts. Use only roles named on the Riverbend page, or say you are assuming one.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

One worked answer. Different learners will notice different details, and that is fine if each one points to something in the scenario.

| # | Worked answer |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Yes.** The app would handle each member's name, address, date of birth, contact details and borrowing history, and children's names, ages, carers' contact details, allergies and photo consent. The Riverbend page lists these under "The information it holds". |
| 2 | **Some of it is sensitive, and some of the people are children.** A borrowing history can show what a person is interested in or worried about, and a long history can hint at health, beliefs or family circumstances. Allergies are health information, and they belong to children. Concession status can point to a person's finances. |
| 3 | **Running the library and, in part, suggesting books.** Loans, renewals and children's sign-up fit the library's purpose. Suggestions use borrowing history in a new way. The scenario does not say whether or how members would be told about this use. That is something the assessment must find out, and a full PIA would examine it. |
| 4 | **Yes, a lot is new.** The information would be collected through a new app, and an outside company would build and host it. Member and children's details would sit on that company's systems, not only on the library's own. The library has less direct control over them. |
| 5 | **Members, carers and children, volunteers and staff, the council and the outside company.** The scenario does not say whether members, carers or the volunteers who help at the desk would be asked for their views. The assessment must find that out. The council runs the library and supplies the network, so it is also a stakeholder. |
| 6 | **Probably many people, and another organisation handles the data.** The page does not give a number of members, so state an assumption, such as "most households in a regional town". The outside company would handle information as well as the library. |

**Decision: Full PIA needed.**

**Why (example):**

1. **The information is detailed and some of it is sensitive.** Borrowing history, allergies and concession status could cause harm if exposed or misused. A threshold assessment cannot settle that. A full PIA can look at each use in turn.
2. **Children are involved.** Carers sign children up, and children may not understand how their information is used.
3. **The project is new and involves an outside company.** A new app and a new vendor mean new places where information can go, and less control over them. A full PIA would map where information goes.
4. **It has not been approved yet.** That is the best time to do the assessment. Changes are cheapest before anything is built or any member's details are shared.

**Responsible person and time frame (example):** the Riverbend page says the library manager and the senior staff make day-to-day decisions about how the library runs, so the library manager could be responsible for the PIA, with the senior staff. The council runs the library, so it is sensible to involve whoever handles privacy at the council. The page does not name that person, so say you are assuming one. The PIA should start before the library commits to the outside company.

**A note on "required" and "recommended".** The OAIC guide says it encourages organisations to do PIAs for projects that involve personal information, and that the legal requirement for "high privacy risk projects" applies to Australian Government agencies (OAIC 2021). So do not rest the decision on that rule. The reasons come from the project: sensitive information, children, a new vendor and a lot of people.

**What counts as correct.** Your conclusion should be "Full PIA needed". "No full PIA needed" and "Not sure" do not fit, because the proposal involves sensitive information, children and an outside company. Do not be concerned if you have fewer reasons than the worked answer.

### Common mistakes

- **Deciding because the library is small or volunteer-run.** The threshold depends on the information and the project, not on the size of the organisation.
- **Stopping at "yes, personal information is involved".** That only starts the check. Sensitivity, change and scale then decide how deep to go.
- **Using facts that are not in the scenario.** Use the Riverbend page and the proposal above. If you need another fact, say that it is an assumption. "A full PIA would check this" is a good sentence.
- **Trying to solve the problems now.** A threshold assessment decides **whether** to do a PIA. It does not find and fix every risk. That is the work of the full PIA.
- **Naming people who are not in the scenario.** Choose an owner from the roles on the Riverbend page, or say you are assuming one.
- **Writing "it depends" and stopping.** Choose one of the three outcomes and give reasons.

## Challenge: Two ways to respond (8 minutes)

A fictional scenario. A volunteer-run community swimming club keeps its registration list in an online spreadsheet. Someone shares the link in a group chat so that anyone with the link can open it. The list has members' names, email addresses and the ages of their children. A parent tells the club president that she opened it by accident.

1. **Name one privacy harm** that could follow, and say whether it is an individual, group or organisational harm (2 minutes).
2. **Compare two options** (3 minutes): quietly lock the spreadsheet and say nothing, or lock it and tell the members. Which would you choose, and why? Name who is affected and how.
3. **Write a practical response** (3 minutes): one action, who does it, by when, and one way to check it worked. Do not invent costs. It must suit a small volunteer club.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

One worked answer.

1. **Harm.** Strangers who open the link could use the email addresses to send scams that look real, because they know the person is a swimming club member. That is an individual harm. For the club as an organisation, the harm is lost trust: members may leave or stop volunteering if they feel the club did not look after their details.
2. **Ethical response.** Locking and telling is the better choice. Members are the ones affected, and only they can act, for example by treating unexpected emails with care. Saying nothing protects the club's image but takes that choice away from them. Telling people costs more discomfort at first, but it respects them.
3. **Practical response.** The president locks the spreadsheet today so that only named committee members can open it, then emails members this week to explain what happened and what to watch for. At the next committee meeting, one committee member checks who has access. This suits a small volunteer club because it relies on a few people's time, not on new software.

## Quick check (5 minutes)

1. Thandi downloads a fitness app to log her runs. It asks for access to her contacts, location and photos. Which action shows her taking responsibility for her own privacy?

   - A. Accepting every request so the app works properly.
   - B. Allowing only what the app needs to log runs, then checking her settings.
   - C. Trusting the app store to have decided that sharing her data is safe.
   - D. Deleting the app after one use, whatever the settings.

2. A school plans an app for a camp. It would record each student's full medical history, home address and photo, although the camp only needs emergency contacts and allergies. After a threshold assessment, what should the school do?

   - A. Skip a PIA, because it is a one-off camp.
   - B. Carry out a full PIA.
   - C. Do a PIA only after the camp, once parents complain.
   - D. Carry on, because parents will sign a consent form.

3. A bookshop plans to count how many people enter on each day, using a door sensor that records only totals. Hamish does a threshold assessment and decides that no full PIA is needed because no personal information is involved. What else should he do?

   - A. Record the project, why no full PIA is needed and who made the decision.
   - B. Nothing, as an assessment with a "no" result does not need to be written down.
   - C. Start a full PIA anyway, to be on the safe side.
   - D. Ask a lawyer to sign off the decision before the project goes ahead.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

1. **B.** Logging runs does not need her contacts or photos, so she should share only what the service needs and check her settings afterwards. Accepting everything gives away more than the service needs. Trusting the app store hands her responsibility to someone else. Deleting the app after one use does not address what she allowed.
2. **B.** The app would collect sensitive information about children, and more of it than the camp needs, so a full PIA is warranted. A one-off camp is still a project that handles personal information. Waiting for complaints is too late. A consent form does not replace checking whether so much information is needed.
3. **A.** The guide says to keep a record of the threshold assessment, including the project description, whether personal information is involved, the decision and who made it. A "no" is still a decision, and writing it down shows the question was asked and answered. A full PIA is unlikely to be needed for a project with no personal information, and the guide does not call for outside sign-off.

## Reflect (5 minutes)

Write 3 to 6 sentences. Do not name other people or share private details such as passwords, card numbers or account details.

- **What privacy means to you.** What is data privacy? In your own words, what does it mean to you? Think about one piece of personal information you would never want shared (for example your health, your location or your messages) and why. Who do you think is responsible for protecting it: you, the organisations that hold it, or both? Is there anything you share online that you would now think twice about?

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: instructor

**Timing (58 minutes).** Teaching text 20 minutes. Exercises 38 minutes: Riverbend threshold assessment 20, two ways to respond 8, quick check 5, discussion 5. Ask learners to read the [Riverbend page](../learners/riverbend.md) before the session, or give 3 minutes at the start of the first challenge.

**Likely misconceptions.**

- Learners think a PIA is only for big projects or big organisations. Ask what a small library would lose if the app leaked children's allergies.
- Learners treat "yes, personal information is involved" as the answer. Ask what happens next in the threshold assessment.
- Learners start fixing the app's risks. Remind them that the threshold assessment only decides whether to do a PIA.
- Learners add facts to Riverbend, such as the number of members. Ask them to say "I am assuming".
- Learners say "it depends". Ask them to choose one of the three outcomes and give reasons.

**Running the discussion.** Use think-pair-share. The question about information learners would never want shared can be personal, so ask pairs to share the *reason* and not the information itself. The answer "both of us" to "who is responsible" is usually correct, so ask what each party's part is.

**Tip.** Collect the pairs' reasons for the Riverbend decision on a board, then group them by sensitivity, children, new technology and the outside company. Ask which reason is strongest.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: keypoints

- Information privacy means knowing what is collected about you, how it is used and who it is shared with, and having a say.
- When privacy fails, harms follow: lost money, stolen identity, embarrassment or unfair treatment, risk of being found, and lost trust. They spread from individuals to groups and organisations.
- Organisations carry most of the duty for privacy, but individuals carry some: share only what a service needs, and care for other people's information.
- A good response is both ethical (who is affected, what is fair) and practical (who does what, by when, and how it is checked).
- A threshold assessment is a quick, written check that decides whether a project needs a full PIA. Sensitivity, children, new technology and outside companies all point towards a full PIA.
