## Responding to identity theft

You met **identity theft** in [Identity, identity management and identity theft](03-identity.md): someone uses your personal details to pretend to be you. This episode is about what to do next.

The order of the first steps matters. Some steps stop money leaving. Others repair the damage over weeks. A good order does the urgent things first, then the repair, then the long wait.

1. **Stop the money.** Contact your bank or card provider, using the number on your card or in the official app.
2. **Secure your accounts.** Change passwords from a device you trust, use a strong, unique password for each account, and turn on multi-factor authentication.
3. **Get support.** IDCARE is Australia and New Zealand's national identity and cyber support service. A case manager can help you make a plan for your situation (IDCARE n.d.).
4. **Check your credit.** A stolen licence or Medicare card could be used to apply for credit. You can ask a credit reporting body for a free credit report, dispute anything you do not recognise and consider a credit ban.
5. **Make a report.** The Australian Signals Directorate (2026) points people to ReportCyber on cyber.gov.au. If it was an online scam, report it to Scamwatch too. If your tax file number is involved, contact the Australian Taxation Office.
6. **Replace stolen documents.** Follow your state or territory's advice. For a passport, tell the Australian Passport Office. A Medicare card is replaced through Services Australia.
7. **Complain, if an organisation mishandled your information.** The OAIC asks you to complain to the organisation first. If it does not respond in a reasonable time (generally 30 days) or does not resolve it, you may be able to complain to the OAIC (OAIC n.d.). Only some organisations are covered by the Privacy Act, as you saw in the last episode. If the organisation is a council-run body, a state or territory regulator may be the right place instead, so check which applies.
8. **Keep checking.** Watch your accounts, mail and messages. Criminals may not use stolen details straight away.

A report does not stop money leaving, so it comes after the urgent steps. Two habits protect you at every step. Use the number on your card, the official app, or a website you type in yourself, and never a number or link from a message or caller. And do not assume an organisation was at fault unless you have a reason to think so.

If your own identity has been stolen, do not wait to finish this episode. Start with your bank, and visit IDCARE and ReportCyber directly by typing the address yourself.

## Cybercrime, cyber war and the future

The rest of this episode is a light look at the wider world. It is here to start your thinking, and you will use the discussion to continue it.

### Cybercrime, cyberterrorism and cyber war

**Cybercrime** is crime that uses computers or networks, or targets them. It ranges from fake invoices to stolen identities. The effects land on people: money lost, months of paperwork, fear, damaged trust, and sometimes delays to care for patients whose records are locked.

**Cyberterrorism** and **cyber war** describe attacks on systems for political goals, or between countries. These are this course's own plain definitions. People define them differently, and experts disagree about where the lines sit. So one of the discussion prompts asks you whether we are at cyber war now, and what you think "war" should mean.

Goodman (2015) writes about how criminals use new technology. The publisher describes the book as a look at how criminals are often early adopters of new technology, with examples such as baby monitors, 3D printers and drones, and as a call for better security. It is not a legal guide. Use it as a prompt to think about the human effects of connected technology.

### Three trends

- **The Internet of Things (IoT)** means everyday objects connected to the internet, such as doorbells, watches and baby monitors. Each one is another device to update, and another way in. A connected device nobody has listed is a device nobody is updating.
- **Big data** means collecting huge amounts of information and finding patterns. The patterns can reveal things about you that you never chose to share, such as a health condition guessed from what you buy.
- **Quantum computing** means a very different kind of computer. Experts warn that it could one day break some of today's encryption. When, and how, is uncertain.

### Who is in charge online?

The internet has no single owner. Governments make laws, and some filter or block parts of it. Platform companies set rules for what you can post and see. And you make choices of your own: settings, tools, and what you share. Where a law or a rule has changed what you can do or see online, you have felt this for yourself.

## Challenge: The stolen wallet (15 minutes)

These are the eight steps, as cards. They are based on the Australian Signals Directorate's page *Recover from identity theft* (2026), IDCARE's website and the OAIC's page on complaining to an organisation.

| Card | Step |
|---|---|
| A | **Contact your bank or card provider.** Use the number on your card or the official app. Ask them to block, freeze or investigate suspicious activity |
| B | **Secure your accounts.** From a device you trust, change passwords, use a strong and unique password for each account, and turn on multi-factor authentication or passkeys |
| C | **Contact IDCARE** (https://www.idcare.org). A case manager can help you make a response plan |
| D | **Check your credit.** Ask a credit reporting body for a free credit report, dispute anything you do not recognise and consider a credit ban |
| E | **Make a report through ReportCyber** (https://www.cyber.gov.au/report-and-recover/report). If it was an online scam, report it to Scamwatch too. If your tax file number is involved, contact the Australian Taxation Office |
| F | **Replace stolen identity documents.** Follow your state or territory's advice. For a passport, tell the Australian Passport Office. A Medicare card is replaced through Services Australia |
| G | **Complain to the organisation that mishandled your information, then to the regulator.** Complain to the organisation first. If it does not respond within a reasonable time (generally 30 days) or does not resolve it, you may complain to the regulator that covers it. Only relevant when an organisation's handling of your information was part of the problem |
| H | **Keep checking.** Watch your accounts, mail and messages for unusual activity, because criminals may not use stolen details straight away |

**Case A: the bus stop (made up).** Mele volunteers at Riverbend on Saturdays. On her way home she puts her bag on a bench at the bus stop and turns to check the timetable. When she turns back, the bag is gone. It held her wallet, with her driver licence, her Medicare card and two bank cards. Within an hour her phone buzzes with a bank text: an online purchase of $480 that she did not make. The bag was taken from a public bench. Nobody, including Riverbend, has been said to have mishandled Mele's information.

1. **Read the cards** (3 minutes).
2. **Put the steps in order** (12 minutes). List the cards you would use, in order, with **one sentence for each** saying why it goes there. Say which card or cards you would **leave out**, and why.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

One worked answer. Your order may differ and still be sound if you give a reason.

| Order | Card | Why it goes here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **A. Bank or card provider** | Money is leaving right now. The bank can block the cards and investigate the $480 charge |
| 2 | **B. Secure your accounts** | The thief holds her cards and documents, and may try to reach her online accounts. Strong passwords and multi-factor authentication make that harder |
| 3 | **C. IDCARE** | A case manager can help her make a plan. She has lost identity documents that could be used to open accounts |
| 4 | **D. Credit check** | A stolen licence and Medicare card could be used to apply for credit. A free credit report may show applications she did not make, and a credit ban may stop new ones |
| 5 | **E. ReportCyber** | A report puts the matter on record with the national system. It does not stop the money leaving, so it comes after the urgent steps |
| 6 | **F. Replace documents** | She needs new documents, but replacing them is less urgent than stopping the thief |
| 7 | **H. Keep checking** | The thief may not use the details straight away, so she should watch her accounts, mail and messages |

**Left out: G.** The bag was taken from a public bench, and no organisation has been said to have mishandled Mele's information. Card G is only relevant when an organisation's handling of the information was part of the problem. The case does not say Riverbend did anything wrong, so do not assume it.

**Accepted alternatives.**

- **A and B** can swap. A is the better start here because money is leaving, but a well-argued B first is accepted.
- **C and E** can swap. IDCARE gives support and a plan. ReportCyber reports to the national system.
- **F** can move earlier if you explain why she needs the document soon. It should not come before A.
- **H** is not a one-off. Writing it as an ongoing step from the start is accepted.

## Challenge: A message from Riverbend (10 minutes)

Use the cards and your answer from the previous challenge.

**Case B: the email (made up).** Rhys is a Riverbend member. He receives an email from the library. It says that someone outside the library accessed its member database, and that his name, address, date of birth and phone number were included. A week later he receives a letter from a lender about a loan application he did not make, and he sees an unfamiliar enquiry on his credit report. The email does not say how the database was accessed.

Say which cards are **added or moved**, and why. Use one or two sentences for each change. Remember that Riverbend is run by a council, so think about which regulator card G would point to.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

One worked answer.

- **G may be added.** The email says someone accessed Riverbend's database, but it does not say how, so do not assume the library was at fault. If Rhys thinks the library's protection of his information was weak, he should complain to Riverbend first. If Riverbend does not respond or resolve it, he may complain to the regulator that covers it. The OAIC deals with complaints about organisations covered by the Privacy Act. A council-run library is generally covered by state or territory privacy law, so the right regulator may be a state or territory one, and the case does not say which state. If you add G, put it **after** the urgent steps, because it takes weeks. If you leave it out and give a reason, for example that the case does not show any fault, that is also accepted.
- **D moves up.** The first signs are a loan application and a credit enquiry, so the credit check and a possible credit ban come earlier. He can dispute anything he does not recognise on the report.
- **A may change.** The case mentions no suspicious bank transactions. He should still contact his bank or card provider if he is worried, but a learner who moves A later and gives a reason is accepted.
- **F may not apply.** The case says only that his details were in the database. No documents are said to be stolen.
- **B, C, E and H stay sensible.** Securing accounts, getting support, reporting and watching for later misuse all still fit.

### Common mistakes

- **Starting with a report to a government service.** Reporting matters, but it does not stop money leaving. Secure money and accounts first.
- **Assuming an organisation is at fault when the case does not say so.** Card G only applies when an organisation's handling of the information is part of the problem.
- **Complaining to a regulator first.** The OAIC asks you to complain to the organisation before you complain to it.
- **Forgetting the step that never ends.** Criminals may use stolen details months later, so keep checking.
- **Using a number or link from a message or caller.** Use the number on your card, the official app, or a website you type in yourself.
- **Treating IDCARE as a police service or a regulator.** It is Australia and New Zealand's national identity and cyber support service. It helps you plan, but it does not make decisions about a complaint.
- **Not giving a reason.** The reason shows you understand why the order matters.

## Quick check (5 minutes)

1. Luca finds that a stolen licence was used to apply for a credit card in his name. The bank has cancelled the application. He wants to make it harder for anyone to open new credit in his name. Which step fits best?

   - A. Change the password on his email account and consider the problem dealt with.
   - B. Contact IDCARE and expect its case manager to withdraw the applications that were made in his name.
   - C. Make a report through ReportCyber and expect it to cancel the other applications automatically.
   - D. Request a free credit report from a credit reporting body and consider a credit ban.

2. A supermarket's loyalty app combines purchase records from millions of customers to guess which shoppers may have a health condition, and uses the guesses for targeted offers. Customers were never asked about their health. Which privacy concern is most direct?

   - A. Customers' purchase records are not personal information, so no privacy concern arises.
   - B. The supermarket is not allowed to analyse purchase records without a customer's written consent.
   - C. The offers might be irrelevant, which is a marketing problem and not a privacy one.
   - D. Patterns in big data can reveal sensitive details that customers never chose to share.

3. A café installs a Wi-Fi security camera and leaves it on the password it came with. What is the main concern?

   - A. None, because a camera only watches and cannot be used to attack anyone.
   - B. It is another connected device that could be an easy way in, so it needs a changed password, updates and a place on the list of devices.
   - C. It is a concern only if the camera records sound.
   - D. It should be replaced every year, whatever its settings.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

1. **D.** A free credit report may show other applications, and a credit ban can make new credit harder to open. A password change does not touch credit, IDCARE helps with a plan but does not withdraw applications, and ReportCyber does not cancel them.
2. **D.** Patterns in big data can reveal details that people never chose to share, such as a health condition. Option A is wrong because purchase records tied to a customer are personal information. Option B overstates the rule, and option C treats a privacy harm as a marketing detail.
3. **B.** Connected devices are another way in, so each one needs a changed password, updates and a place on the list of devices someone looks after. A camera that connects to Wi-Fi can be attacked, and the concern does not depend on sound or on replacing it yearly.

## Reflect (5 minutes)

Choose one prompt (or the one your instructor gives you) and write 3 to 6 sentences. Do not name other people or share private details such as passwords, card numbers or account details.

> **Content note:** the prompt about identity theft may be upsetting, for example if it has happened to you or someone close to you. You can answer it in general terms or choose another. IDCARE offers support, and it can help to talk to someone you trust.

- **Who is in charge online?** Who do you think controls the internet, and where do you think the legal boundaries are? Think about governments, companies that run platforms, and your own choices. Where have you seen a law or a rule change what you can do or see online?
- **If it happened to you.** Where would you go if you knew you had experienced identity theft? Who would you contact first, and who next? Which step would you find hardest, and why?
- **Are we at cyber war?** Are we at cyber war now? There is no single agreed definition, so say what you think the word "war" should mean before you answer. What effect could attacks on systems have on ordinary people, such as people trying to get health care?
- **New technology and you.** Choose **one or two** of these, and say what each could mean for your own life.
  - What are the implications of the Internet of Things (IoT), where everyday objects are connected to the internet?
  - How could big data affect you, for example through what companies could work out from what you buy or search?
  - What impact could quantum computing have, remembering that experts are unsure about when and how?

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: instructor

**Timing (55 minutes).** Teaching text 20 minutes. Exercises 35 minutes: stolen wallet 15, message from Riverbend 10, quick check 5, discussion 5. The wider-world section is a light overview, so do not spend long on it in the teaching time. The discussion is where it gets airtime. Ask learners to read the [Riverbend page](../learners/riverbend.md) before the session, or give 2 minutes at the start.

**Likely misconceptions.**

- Learners start with a report to a government service. Ask what stops the money leaving.
- Learners assume an organisation was at fault in Case A or B. Ask which sentence in the case says so.
- Learners treat IDCARE as police or a regulator.
- Learners complain to a regulator before the organisation. Check the order on card G.
- Learners say Riverbend's regulator is "the OAIC" without thinking about the council. Ask who runs the library and which law that points to.
- Learners treat "cyber war" as one agreed idea. Ask them to define "war" first.

**Content note.** Identity theft is personal for some learners. Do not ask anyone for their own story. Let them do the exercise as pure ordering and skip the "why" sentences if they prefer. Keep IDCARE visible.

**Running the discussion.** Use think-pair-share, with one prompt each for the pairs so that all the topics get covered. Give each pair a different prompt, then ask each pair for one sentence. For the cyber war prompt, ask pairs who disagree to swap definitions of "war". For the new technology prompt, ask what each trend adds that the learner could change today, such as a router password.

**Tip.** Write the eight card letters on cards or sticky notes and let pairs physically order them. Ask them to move a card when Case B is read out.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: keypoints

- In identity theft, stop the money first, secure your accounts, get support, check your credit, make a report, replace documents and keep checking.
- A complaint comes after the urgent steps. Complain to the organisation first, then to the regulator that covers it, and only if the organisation's handling was part of the problem.
- Use the number on your card, the official app or a website you type in yourself, never a number or link from a message or caller.
- Cybercrime lands on people, and experts disagree about where cyber war begins, so define your terms before you argue.
- Connected devices, big data and quantum computing each change risk in a different way: another device to update, patterns you never chose to share, and a future threat to some encryption.
