## What is vetting?

**Vetting** means checking a person before you give them trust and access. An ordinary employer might confirm identity, check that qualifications are real, call referees and, for some roles, check criminal history. For roles that handle highly sensitive information, such as in government, the checks go deeper and are repeated.

In Australia, the Australian Government's Protective Security Policy Framework (PSPF) sets protective security expectations for government agencies. It includes personnel security, which is deciding who is suitable to be given access and keeping that decision under review (Protective Security Policy Framework, n.d.). The framework is written for government agencies, not for a small business. It is useful because it shows how a large organisation describes the idea that trust is checked before access is given, and then checked again. A link is in Go deeper below.

What can vetting do? It lowers the chance of hiring someone who is not who they say they are, or who has a record that matters for the job. That is valuable. But it has limits.

## What vetting cannot do

- **It is a snapshot.** A check shows what was true on the day it was done. People's lives change. A person with a clean record can later be in financial trouble, become angry at their employer, or be pressured by someone else.
- **It only finds what has been recorded.** A clean result means nothing has been recorded against the person. It does not mean they will behave well.
- **It cannot read intentions.**
- **It can be uneven.** Checks can be skipped for people who already seem trusted, and that is when they matter. Checks must also be fair. They should be proportionate to the access the role gives. Checking a gardener as hard as a finance officer is a waste, and may be unfair.

Many organisations also ask staff to report changes that might matter for the role, such as a new outside job. They treat these reports fairly and privately. The idea is that trust is not given once and then forgotten. This is called **ongoing assessment**.

This is why good organisations treat vetting as **one layer**. After the check they keep reviewing, they listen to staff, and they limit access.

## Compartmentalisation

Think of the compartments in a ship: sealed sections, so that if one floods the whole ship does not sink. In security, **compartmentalisation** means limiting each person's access to systems, and their knowledge, to what their job needs. You may remember least privilege from [Identity, identity management and identity theft](03-identity.md). Compartmentalisation is the wider idea. It covers who can open what, who knows what, and how systems are kept apart.

There are three kinds.

| Kind | Idea | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Access** | Each person can open only what their job needs. | The accounts team can open the accounts folder, and not the staff records. |
| **Knowledge** | Each person knows only the part of the work they need. | A café chain sends each branch a pre-mixed base for its signature sauce, so no single branch knows the whole recipe. |
| **Systems** | Systems are kept apart, so a problem in one does not spread freely to another. | A business keeps its guest Wi-Fi on a different network from its customer files. |

### Why it helps

You cannot predict every mistake or every betrayal. Vetting is a snapshot, and awareness can fail on a bad day. Compartments **limit the damage** when something goes wrong. If one person's account is taken over, or one person turns bad, they can reach only their own compartment, and not everything.

### What it costs

Compartments have costs.

- People may need to ask for access, and work may slow down.
- A person who is the only one with access becomes a single point of failure when they are sick or on leave.

So plan for it. Decide who can cover, and have a recorded, approved way to get extra access in an emergency. Review who has access at set times, because compartments weaken as people change jobs. A simple record helps: for each system or folder, who owns it, who may open it, and when that was last checked. If nobody can say who owns a folder, nobody is looking after who can open it.

### Why people need to understand it

Compartments work best when people understand why they exist. If staff think access limits mean they are distrusted, they look for ways around them. If they know the limit protects them too, because a problem cannot be pinned on someone who could never have reached the data, they are more likely to accept it. This links to [People at work: BYOD and working from home](08-people-at-work.md), where a rule's burden pushed people towards shortcuts.

## A case to work on

The next two challenges use a short fictional case, so that you can see the limits of vetting in practice. Use only the text below. Where it does not say something, write "the scenario does not say", or use "could" or "may".

*Larkspur Marine is a fictional engineering firm. It gives a new systems administrator full access to every server on his first day, because he has a clearance. Six months later, he copies a large part of the customer database to a personal device. The firm's hiring check noticed an unexplained gap in his work history, and nobody followed it up.*

The scenario does not say what the clearance covered, what the gap was, whether anyone reviewed his access during the six months, how the copying was found, or why he did it. Do not guess. The questions are about controls, not about judging him.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: challenge

## Challenge: What did the checks miss? (10 minutes)

Read the case (2 minutes). Then answer in two to four sentences (8 minutes).

**What did the checks miss, or fail to catch?** Name **two** points from the case, and say for each why a check could not have caught it, or why it may not have been followed up.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Worked answer

Any two points that the case supports are accepted. Here are three.

- **A noticed gap that nobody followed up.** The hiring check found an unexplained gap in his work history, and nobody followed it up. That is a check that found something and then stopped. The scenario does not say how large the gap was or what it hid, so a good answer does not claim that the gap explains what happened later. It only says that a flag was raised and not answered.
- **A clearance is a snapshot.** He was given full access because he has a clearance. A clearance shows that someone was judged suitable at one point in time. It cannot show what he would decide six months later. The scenario does not say what the clearance covered.
- **No review after day one.** The scenario does not say whether anyone reviewed his access or his behaviour in the six months. A good answer says the case is silent, and not that no review existed. It can still say that nothing in the case shows one, and that a review **could** have raised the alarm earlier.

**Checking yourself.** You used the case only. You said "the scenario does not say" or "could" where it is silent. You gave two points, each with a reason.

## Challenge: Limit the reach (10 minutes)

Use the same case. Answer in two to four sentences each. Use "could" or "may" for anything the case does not state.

1. **How might compartmentalisation have changed the outcome?** Choose **one** kind (access, knowledge or systems), say what the limit would have been, and say how it could have reduced the amount one person could reach.
2. **What is the cost of that limit?** A person in a systems role may need wide access to do the job. Name **one cost or difficulty** of limiting it, and one way to reduce the cost.
3. **Which one people-control would you recommend?** It can be an awareness activity, a rule or an access limit. Say who would own it. The owner should be a role, not "all staff" or "management".

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Worked answer

**1. Compartmentalisation (one kind is enough).**

- *Access.* He had full access to every server on his first day. If his role had given him only the servers his tasks needed, the customer database may not have been among them, or a smaller part of it **could** have been reachable. The scenario does not say what his tasks were, so a good answer says "could".
- *Systems.* If the customer database had sat in a separate system that needed separate approval, a general administrator account would not have opened it. The scenario does not say how the firm's systems were arranged.
- *Knowledge.* This fits the case less well, because the case is about what he could reach, and not about what he knew. A good answer can say so.

Do not say that compartmentalisation would have **prevented** the copying. The case does not show that. Say it **could have reduced** how much he could reach.

**2. The cost.** A systems administrator may need to reach many servers to fix problems. If access is split too tightly, urgent fixes slow down, and people may look for shortcuts. That is the burden from [People at work](08-people-at-work.md). Ways to reduce the cost: split the administrator job into separate roles, give extra access only for a task and take it away afterwards, record when extra access is used, or use a two-person rule for the most sensitive actions (you met this in [The insider threat](04-insider-threat.md)).

**3. One people-control (examples).**

- *Rule.* Access to the customer database needs a second person's approval, and the database's owner reviews use each month. **Owner:** the head of IT security.
- *Review.* Check the access and work history of anyone in a trusted role at set times, and not only at hiring, and follow up every flag raised in a hiring check. **Owner:** the HR manager for the follow-up, and the head of IT security for the access review.
- *Rule.* New starters get the access their role needs on day one, and more only when their manager asks for it. **Owner:** the IT service manager.

**Checking yourself.** In question 1 you named a kind of limit and said how it could reduce the reach, without claiming it would have stopped everything. In question 2 you gave a cost and a fix. In question 3 you named a control and an owner.

### Common mistakes

- **Guessing why he did it.** The scenario gives no reason. The task is about controls, not about his motives.
- **Stating what the scenario does not say as fact.** It does not say what the gap was, what the clearance covered, or whether anyone reviewed his access. Use "the scenario does not say", "could" or "may".
- **Saying "vetting failed completely".** The check noticed the gap. The failure was that nobody followed it up, and that a clearance alone was treated as enough.
- **Saying compartmentalisation would have prevented it.** It may have limited how much he could reach.
- **Forgetting the cost.** Limits slow people down. A good plan says how to reduce the burden.
- **Adding facts from outside the case.** Use the text only, so that your answers can be checked.
- **Suggesting a control with no owner.** Every control needs a person or role responsible.

## Quick check (5 minutes)

1. A firm checks a new warehouse manager's identity, licence and referees, and everything is clean. On his first day, it gives him access to every system, because he has been checked. What is the main problem?

   - A. Vetting is one layer among several, so his access should still match what the job needs.
   - B. The check should also have included his school results, his hobbies and his social media accounts outside work.
   - C. A clean check means the firm can rely on him, so full access is reasonable.
   - D. Warehouse managers should be given access in stages over their first year.

2. A company asks staff who approve supplier payments to tell HR if a close relative starts working for a supplier. It promises to treat each report privately and fairly. What is the main purpose?

   - A. To build a file that lets the company dismiss staff more quickly.
   - B. To keep trust under review after hiring, so a risk from a change can be discussed early.
   - C. To replace the checks that were made when the staff were first hired.
   - D. To make sure staff keep an eye on each other and report any odd behaviour.

3. A game studio is making a game it has not announced. It briefs each contractor only on the level they are building, and not on the whole story. What benefit does this give the studio most directly?

   - A. Contractors can be approved more quickly, with less paperwork for each one.
   - B. The studio can stop checking who its contractors are, since each one knows so little.
   - C. The game will be of better quality overall, because each person can focus on one part.
   - D. If one contractor leaks or loses their files, only part of the game is exposed.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

1. **A.** A clean check shows the past, not what he will decide later, so access should still be limited to what the job needs. Option B would be an unfair and disproportionate check. Option C treats vetting as the only layer. Option D is not the main problem, because the issue is that access does not match the job at all.
2. **B.** This is ongoing assessment: trust is not checked once and forgotten. It works only if staff believe they will be treated fairly. Option A is wrong because reports handled privately and fairly are meant to support staff and the company, not to prepare dismissals. Option C is wrong because reports add to the first checks and do not replace them. Option D is wrong because the reports are about a person's own changes, not about watching colleagues.
3. **D.** Compartmentalising knowledge means that no single person holds the whole picture, so one leak or loss does limited harm. Option A is wrong because briefing contractors separately does not speed up approval. Option B is wrong because limits on knowledge are one layer, and they do not replace checks. Option C is wrong because quality is not what limits on knowledge are for.

## Reflect (5 minutes)

Choose one or both prompts and write 3 to 6 sentences for each. Do not name private individuals or share private details such as passwords.

- **When checks fail.** You worked on one case. What real cases do you know of, or have you heard of, in which an organisation's checks on a person did not prevent harm, and what did they lead to? (If you cannot name one, think about what you would want an organisation to change after such a case.) Which kind of change do you think matters most: better checks on people, limits on what each person can reach, or better awareness for everyone? Why?
- **Vetting and its limits.** Australia has vetting requirements, for example for people who handle sensitive government information. Why do you think problems still happen, even where checks are strict? What does that tell you about how an organisation should treat its own staff, in both trust and in limits?

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: instructor

**Timing (55 minutes).** Teaching text 25 minutes. Exercises 30 minutes: what did the checks miss 10, limit the reach 10, quick check 5, discussion 5. The Larkspur Marine case is short and is read inside the first challenge.

**Handling the case.** The case, Larkspur Marine, is fictional. Keep learners to the text: where it is silent, they say "the scenario does not say" or use "could" or "may". If learners bring up real cases they know of, keep the focus on controls ("what could have limited the reach of one person?") rather than on whether the person was right, and treat details offered from memory as unverified.

**Likely misconceptions.**

- Learners say vetting "failed completely" or that it is useless. The point is that a check is a snapshot and one layer.
- Learners add facts the case does not give. Ask them to use "could" or "may", or to say the scenario does not say.
- Learners say compartmentalisation would have prevented the whole event. Ask what it could have reduced.
- Learners forget the cost to staff, or suggest a control with no owner.
- Learners think strict checks mean no risk. This links to the second discussion prompt.

**Running the discussion.** Use think-pair-share (see the general notes). Ask a pair that disagrees about which change matters most to explain their reasons. The strongest answers say that the three changes work together.

**Tip.** Ask pairs to draw a one-line ship on the board and mark which compartment their chosen control would seal.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: keypoints

- Vetting checks a person before you give them trust and access. It lowers risk, and it is valuable.
- A check is a snapshot. It finds only what was recorded, and it cannot read intentions, so vetting is one layer and not the whole answer.
- Ongoing assessment keeps trust under review after hiring, and it works only when staff are treated fairly and privately.
- Compartmentalisation limits access, knowledge and systems to what each job needs, so one mistake or one bad decision does limited harm.
- Limits have costs for staff, so plan cover and emergency access, review who has access, explain why, and give every control an owner.
