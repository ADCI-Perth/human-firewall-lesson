## Your profile is a puzzle

One post is rarely dangerous. A whole profile is different, because an attacker can lay the pieces side by side. In [Social engineering: hacking the human](02-social-engineering.md) you met the research stage of an attack, where the attacker quietly collects facts. Social media is a very good research tool for a criminal, and we fill it in ourselves, for free.

Think about what a public profile can show.

- **Facts:** your name, your suburb, where you work.
- **Patterns:** when you post from the gym, or which café you visit on Fridays.
- **Relationships:** who your family, friends and manager are.
- **Feelings:** whether you are happy at work or fed up.
- **Answers:** details that sound like the answers to security questions, such as a first pet or a first school.

Each piece looks harmless. Together, they let someone write a message that sounds as if it came from your manager, guess how to reset your password, or work out when your house is empty.

You are not the only one who decides what appears. Other people post about you. A friend tags you at a party, a club posts a group photo, a colleague mentions where you work. The National Cyber Security Centre in the United Kingdom, the NCSC, advises you to be aware of what others say about you online, because criminals can use it for identity theft or more convincing phishing (NCSC 2022). The same goes for what you post about others: check with them first.

### What you can do

- **Check who can see your posts, and share only what is needed.** Most platforms let you choose between everyone, friends or a smaller group. The NCSC advises managing your security and privacy settings (NCSC 2022).
- **Turn on two-step verification.** It asks for a second proof when you log in, such as a code sent to your phone, so a stolen password alone is not enough. It is the same idea as multi-factor authentication in [Identity, identity management and identity theft](03-identity.md).
- **Think about your digital footprint.** That is the trail of information about you that exists online, including what other people post about you.
- **Ask before you post:** could a stranger use this?

## Fakes, hijacks and personal attacks

### Fake accounts

Social software makes it easy to pretend. The NCSC suggests checking when an account was set up, whether the name looks random, and whether the follower count fits, because followers can be bought. It adds that verification badges can help you identify real accounts (NCSC 2022). If you are not sure, do not accept the request, and use the platform's report button.

### Hijacked accounts

A real friend's account can also be taken over. If a contact suddenly posts something out of character, the NCSC advises checking with them by another method, such as a phone call, in case the account was hacked (NCSC 2022). Do not ask in the same chat. That is where the attacker is waiting, and they may answer.

### Personal attacks

Then there are attacks on people.

- **Online harassment** is repeated, unwanted or abusive behaviour aimed at a person.
- **Impersonation** is someone posting as you to damage your name.
- **Doxxing** is collecting a person's private details, such as a home address or workplace, and publishing them so that others can find them. It often starts with the same joining of small pieces you have just read about.

The harm is real: fear, lost sleep, and sometimes a person no longer feels safe at home.

If it happens to you, a few steps help.

1. **Do not argue** with the attackers.
2. **Save evidence**, such as screenshots showing dates and usernames.
3. **Report** the posts to the platform.
4. **Tell someone you trust**, and tell your employer if work is involved.
5. If someone is impersonating you, **warn the people who might be fooled**, using your real account.

In Australia, the eSafety Commissioner provides information on reporting serious online abuse (eSafety Commissioner n.d.). If you feel physically threatened, contact the police. If you see it happening to someone else, do not share it or pile on. Report it, and check in with the person.

## Challenge: What does the profile show? (12 minutes)

**The profile.** **Jonty Pell** (fictional). Bio: "Numbers by day, sourdough by night. Marrow Creek. Dog dad to Biscuit. Bookkeeper at Greywater Timber Supplies. Marrow Creek High, class of 2014." The profile is public. Anyone can see it.

| # | Post | Date |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Turning 30 on the 14th! Drinks at the Railway Hotel, Saturday 6 pm, all welcome." | 3 Nov |
| 2 | "Month-end again. Payroll goes out Thursday and I'm the only one who knows the bank portal. Denise says no pressure. Ha." | 5 Nov |
| 3 | Check-in: "Marrow Creek Fitness, 6:10 am. Monday, Wednesday and Friday regulars, you know who you are." | 7 Nov |
| 4 | "Twelve days in Portugal! Flying out Friday. The house will be empty, ha. Biscuit is off to my sister Bree's." | 9 Nov |
| 5 | "Throwback to Gerald, my first car, a yellow 2004 Corolla. And Pepper, my first ever pet, a very grumpy cat." | 11 Nov |
| 6 | "Happy birthday to my mum, Linda Pell (née Ferris)! Love you." | 12 Nov |
| 7 | "Third change to the invoice process this term. LedgerLark is driving me up the wall." | 14 Nov |
| 8 | "Passed over for supervisor again. Maybe it's time for something new." | 16 Nov |
| 9 | Photo of a freshly painted door: "Finally painted the front door at no. 14! Wattle Street's sunniest door." | 18 Nov |
| 10 | Photo tagged by a colleague: "Greywater Timber team night out. Thanks Denise Halloran for dinner!" | 20 Nov |

1. **Read the profile** (2 minutes).
2. **List what it states** (4 minutes). Write at least **eight things the profile says outright**, with the post number for each.
3. **Join the pieces** (6 minutes). Write at least **three things an attacker could work out** by joining two or more items. For each, say which post numbers you joined. Use "could" or "may" for anything the profile does not say outright.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

One worked answer. Yours may differ.

**Stated (at least eight needed).** His name, town and job as a bookkeeper at Greywater Timber Supplies (bio). His school and the year he finished, 2014 (bio). That he turns 30 on the 14th, with the party venue and time (post 1). That he is the only one who knows the payroll bank portal, that payroll goes out on Thursday, and that someone called Denise has told him there is no pressure (post 2). That he is at Marrow Creek Fitness at 6:10 am on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays (post 3). That he is away for 12 days from a Friday, that the house will be empty and that his dog is with his sister Bree (post 4). His first car and first pet (post 5). His mother's name and maiden name (post 6). The software his employer uses, LedgerLark, and his frustration with it (post 7). That he was passed over for supervisor (post 8). His house number and street (post 9). The full name of a colleague, Denise Halloran (post 10; the posts do not say whether she is his manager).

**What an attacker could work out (at least three needed).**

| What an attacker could work out | Posts joined |
|---|---|
| His approximate year of birth | Bio (class of 2014) and post 1 (turning 30) |
| His street address: no. 14 Wattle Street, Marrow Creek | Post 9 (number and street) and the bio (town) |
| That the house at that address will be empty for 12 days from the Friday | Posts 4 and 9 |
| That he has a set early-morning routine, so he is out on those days | Post 3 |
| That a fake payroll message that appears to come from a colleague, such as Denise Halloran, could fit his real week | Posts 2 and 10 (and 7 for the software name) |
| That he may be open to a job offer | Posts 7 and 8 |
| Possible answers to account-recovery questions: first pet (Pepper), first car (a yellow 2004 Corolla), mother's maiden name (Ferris), school | Bio, posts 5 and 6 |

The profile does not say whether he lives alone, whether anyone else is in the house, or what his bank or email providers are. Any statement about those would be a guess, so a good answer says "could" or leaves them out.

## Challenge: How could it be misused, and how do you protect it? (13 minutes)

Use Jonty's profile from the previous challenge.

1. **Show how it could be misused** (9 minutes). Choose **three** of these misuse types, one row each:
   - (a) guessing the answers to account-recovery questions, or guessing passwords;
   - (b) writing a convincing fake message to Jonty or his workplace;
   - (c) a real-world risk, such as to Jonty's home or belongings;
   - (d) approaching Jonty with a believable offer or story;
   - (e) a personal attack, such as harassment or publishing his address (skip this one if you would rather).

   For each, write what the attacker would **do**, which **post numbers** they would join (at least two), and **who could be hurt**.
2. **Say how to protect it** (4 minutes). Write **three changes** Jonty could make, and **one thing** Greywater Timber Supplies could do to help its staff.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

One worked answer. Any three misuse types that you can support with two or more post numbers are sound.

| Type | What the attacker would do | Posts joined | Who could be hurt |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Recovery answers | If Jonty's accounts use questions such as "first pet" or "mother's maiden name", try Pepper, Gerald or Ferris, and use his birthday and school to guess passwords. Then take over an account. | Bio, 1, 5, 6 | Jonty (his accounts), and anyone who trusts messages from them |
| (b) Fake message | Send Jonty an email that appears to come from Denise Halloran (who could be a colleague or manager; the posts do not say), saying "payroll is urgent, please change this staff member's bank account in LedgerLark today". Jonty says he is the only one who knows the portal, so a fake request could seem to fit his real week. | 2, 7, 10 | Staff, who might be paid wrongly; Greywater, which could lose money; Jonty, who may be blamed |
| (c) Real world | Know that the house at no. 14 Wattle Street, Marrow Creek, will be empty for 12 days from a Friday. | 4, 9, bio | Jonty (his belongings and his home) |
| (d) Believable offer | Contact him as a "recruiter" with a story about a better job, knowing he feels passed over. The attacker could ask him for documents "to apply", or for access. | 7, 8 | Jonty and Greywater, if he shares work information |
| (e) Personal attack | Collect his address and workplace and publish them to harass him, which is doxxing. Someone angry about something at his workplace could do this. | 9, 10, bio | Jonty, his family and his colleagues, who may feel unsafe |

**Protection.**

- **Jonty could:** set his posts to friends only; post holiday photos after he is home; leave out his street and house number; not post answers that sound like security questions (first pet, first car, mother's maiden name); keep complaints about work for a private talk with his manager; ask colleagues to check before tagging him.
- **Greywater could:** give staff short, specific rules on what not to share (for example payroll roles, software names and anything confidential), explain the reasons, and make it easy to ask questions. A second person should approve any change to payroll details, so one fake message cannot move money. This links to two-person approval in [The insider threat](04-insider-threat.md).

### Common mistakes

- **Listing facts only.** The point is the joining. "He goes to the gym" is a fact. "He is out of the house at 6 am on set days" is what an attacker could work out.
- **Stating guesses as facts.** The profile does not say whether Jonty lives alone or which bank he uses. Write "could" or "may" for what is not stated.
- **Citing only one post.** A misuse that uses one post is not a profile puzzle. Join at least two.
- **Blaming Jonty.** He is an ordinary person sharing normal life. Say what could be done to reduce the risk, not that he was foolish.
- **Choosing only technical attacks.** The profile gives an attacker facts about people. Most of the misuse is about believable stories and guesses, not hacking.
- **"He should delete everything."** A good answer keeps what is useful about sharing and changes who sees it, when and how much.
- **Using a real profile.** Do not practise on a real person, even if their posts are public.

## Quick check (5 minutes)

1. Zainab shares photos of her weekend hikes with a few close friends, but her profile is set to public. Which step best reduces what a stranger can learn about her, while she keeps posting?

   - A. Turn off comments on her photos.
   - B. Change the audience to friends only, and check who is on that list.
   - C. Take photos without her face in them.
   - D. Rely on the platform to hide anything private.

2. Sunita gets a connection request on a professional networking site from someone who says they supply her company's printers. The profile was set up last week, its name is a random string of letters, and it has thousands of followers but no posts. What does this most clearly show?

   - A. A new account, a random-looking name and a follower count that does not fit.
   - B. A verification badge and a long work history.
   - C. A friendly photo and a polite message.
   - D. A shared connection and a company logo.

3. Brendan belongs to a cycling club's group chat. A fellow member's account suddenly posts a link with the message "Is this you in this video??". The member has never posted anything like this before. What is the best response?

   - A. Click the link quickly, to see if the video shows him.
   - B. Reply in the group chat and ask whether the account has been hacked.
   - C. Leave the link alone, and contact the member another way, such as by phone.
   - D. Forward the link on to the other members, so they can say whether it is safe.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::: solution

## Solution

1. **B.** Settings decide who can see a post. Turning off comments, hiding her face or relying on the platform's defaults does not change the audience.
2. **A.** These are the things the NCSC suggests checking, and together they suggest a fake account. A verification badge, a friendly message or a shared logo would point the other way, and a polite message proves nothing.
3. **C.** A link from a hijacked account is the attacker's way in, so do not click it. The NCSC advises checking with a contact by another method if they post something out of character, in case their account was hacked. Asking in the group chat may reach the attacker, who may even answer. Forwarding the link spreads the risk to more people.

## Reflect (5 minutes)

Write 3 to 6 sentences. Do not name other people or share private details such as passwords, card numbers, account details or your address. If any of this is close to something that has happened to you, write only what you are comfortable with.

- **Social media and your own information.** Do you think social media is really safe? What makes you say that? How do you protect your own personal data online, and is there one habit you would change after this episode? Why do people share personal information, for example on Instagram, even when they know the risks? Think about what you get from sharing, and what it might cost you. Some countries control which social media their people can use. What do you think of that: when might it protect people, and when might it harm them?

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: instructor

**Timing (55 minutes).** Teaching text 20 minutes. Exercises 35 minutes: what the profile shows 12, misuse and protection 13, quick check 5, discussion 5.

**Content note.** Say at the start that the episode mentions harassment and doxxing in general terms. Tell learners that skipping option (e) is fine. Do not ask anyone to share personal experiences. If a learner is upset, point them to the [eSafety Commissioner](https://www.esafety.gov.au/report) and, in your own setting, to your usual support.

**Likely misconceptions.**

- Learners list facts and stop. Ask them to point to two posts and say what the pair shows that neither shows alone.
- Learners write guesses as facts, such as "he lives alone". Ask where the profile says it, and show them "could".
- Learners blame Jonty. Ask what a reasonable person would think about a birthday post.
- Learners suggest "delete everything". Ask what Jonty would lose, and whether a smaller audience would do.
- Learners want to look up a real person to make the task "realistic". Say no, and explain that profiling a real person is not an acceptable practice exercise.

**Running the discussion.** Use think-pair-share. The prompt asks about personal habits, so ask pairs to share the *reason* behind a habit and not the details of their accounts. The last question, about why people share despite the risks, tends to produce the best talk: ask what each reason gets the sharer and who pays the cost. For the question about countries that control social media, ask who decides what "protect" means.

**Tip.** Run the profile challenge with sticky notes. Give each pair ten post numbers on a board and ask them to draw lines between posts that join. The pattern of lines shows how much more a pair says than a single post.

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: keypoints

- One post is a piece. A whole profile is the picture, and an attacker can join facts, patterns, relationships, feelings and answers to build a believable story.
- Other people's posts about you count too, so check before you post about them.
- Cut down what a profile gives away: choose the audience, share only what is needed, use two-step verification, and think about who could use a post.
- Check a suspect account's age, name and follower count. If a friend's account acts oddly, check with them another way, not in the same chat.
- If you are attacked online: do not argue, save evidence, report, tell someone, and warn others if you are being impersonated.
